## 6.2 The linear representation hypothesis

**Aim.** The reader can state the linear representation hypothesis (models represent many concepts as directions in activation space), knows the main evidence for it, and knows a documented exception.

**Text (draft).**

> The linear representation hypothesis says that a model represents many concepts as directions in its activation space. How strongly a concept is present in an input corresponds to how far the activation vector extends along that concept's direction, which a dot product measures.
>
> The evidence comes from several places. Word-embedding analogies (3.2) are an early case. In language models, a single direction separates true statements from false ones across many topics (Marks and Tegmark 2023). In a transformer trained to play the board game Othello, the board state can be read off linearly once it is described as "my piece" and "the opponent's piece" rather than "black" and "white" (Nanda, Lee, and Wattenberg 2023). Refusal in chat models is controlled largely by a single direction (Arditi et al. 2024). Park, Choe, and Veitch (2023) give a formal statement of the hypothesis.
>
> The hypothesis does not hold for everything. Some features are represented nonlinearly: for example, days of the week and months of the year are arranged on a circle in a two-dimensional subspace (Engels et al. 2024). The hypothesis matters because most interpretability tools (probes, sparse autoencoders, steering) assume it. Where it holds, a model's concepts can be found with linear algebra.

**Image.** A 2D projection of one layer's activations for true and false statements, with the separating direction drawn as an arrow; an inset showing the seven weekday tokens arranged on a circle.

**Interactive: "Find the direction".**

- Display: activations for about 400 true and false statements, projected to 2D; a histogram of their projections onto the current direction.
- Controls: projection (principal components, random, difference of class means); a direction arrow the reader can drag; dataset (true/false statements; weekday tokens); train-on-one-topic, test-on-another switch.
- Try: (1) Drag the arrow until the histogram splits true from false. (2) Compare your direction with the difference of class means. (3) Switch to the weekday tokens: can one direction capture "day of the week"?
- What to notice: one direction captures truth surprisingly well across topics; the weekdays need a circle.
- Build: precomputed activations from an open model; medium.

**Check.** If a concept is represented linearly, what operation reads it out? Answer: a dot product with the concept's direction.

**Links.** Back to 2.3, 3.2; forward to 6.3 to 6.6, 7.2. References: Park, Choe, and Veitch (2023); Marks and Tegmark (2023), "The Geometry of Truth"; Nanda, Lee, and Wattenberg (2023); Engels et al. (2024), "Not All Language Model Features Are Linear".
