## 6.1 What interpretability tries to do

**Aim.** The reader can sort interpretability methods by what they access (behavior only, activations, weights) and by the kind of evidence they give (correlational or causal), and knows why the field prizes causal evidence.

**Text (draft).**

> Interpretability is the study of what a trained network has learned and how it computes its outputs. Black-box methods use only inputs and outputs. White-box methods also read the network's internals: its activations (the numbers computed at each layer for a given input) and its weights. Mechanistic interpretability aims at the most detailed level: an account of the algorithm a network uses, stated in terms of the network's parts.
>
> Methods also differ in the evidence they give. Observing that an activation pattern accompanies a concept gives correlational evidence: the information is present in the network. Changing the activation and observing a change in behavior gives causal evidence: the network uses the information. A classifier trained on activations (a probe, 6.3) gives the first kind. Steering (6.6) and activation patching (6.7) give the second.
>
> For safety, interpretability is meant to answer questions that behavior cannot settle: whether a model represents facts it does not report, which goals or rules of thumb drive its outputs, and whether it behaves differently when it believes it is being tested.

**Image.** A grid with two rows (correlational, causal) and three columns (behavior, activations, weights), with methods placed in cells: behavioral evaluations; probes and sparse autoencoders; steering and activation patching; weight-based circuit analysis.

**Interactive: "Methods map".**

- Display: the grid as a clickable diagram. Each method opens a card: what it accesses, what it can show, a one-line example, its main limitation, and a link to its page.
- Quiz mode: given a research claim (for example, "the model represents whether a statement is true" or "the model uses that representation when it answers"), the reader picks which methods could support it and whether the support would be correlational or causal.
- Try: (1) Work through four claims in quiz mode. (2) Find a claim that no method in the correlational row could support.
- What to notice: "the information is there" and "the model uses it" are different claims that need different evidence.
- Build: live; small.

**Check.** A probe decodes "the input is in French" from layer 6 with 99% accuracy. Does the model use that information? Answer: not shown; that requires a causal test.
