## 1.1 The timeline

**Aim.** The reader can place about 35 milestones on one time axis and sees the recurring pattern: most core ideas are decades older than their success, which waited on computing power and data.

**Text (draft).**

> Most of the ideas behind today's AI are old. The perceptron, a single artificial neuron that learns, dates from 1958. Backpropagation, the method used to train networks with many layers, was worked out by 1970 and became widely known in 1986. Convolutional networks read handwritten digits in 1989. None of these dominated AI until after 2012, when graphics processors (GPUs) and large labeled datasets made it practical to train large networks.
>
> Before then, funding and attention rose and fell twice. The two collapses are called the AI winters, roughly 1974 to 1980 and 1987 to 1993. Each followed a period in which public claims ran well ahead of what the systems could do.
>
> The timeline has three lanes: ideas, computing power and data, and the funding climate. Many events come with a well-known story. Where the story differs from what the documents show, the event's card shows both: the lore and the record.

**Image.** The tool itself is the image. Print and fallback version: one horizontal axis from 1940 to 2025 with the two winters shaded and 12 key events labeled.

**Interactive: "Timeline".**

- Display: three horizontal lanes (ideas; computing power and data; funding climate) on a zoomable axis from 1940 to 2025, with the winters shaded. Each event is a card.
- Controls: zoom and pan; filter chips (architectures, training methods, data and hardware, games and benchmarks, funding and policy); a lore/record switch on cards that have both. A card opens a panel with the date, one sentence on what happened, one on why it mattered, and a link to the page that explains the idea.
- Try: (1) Measure the gap between the first derivation of backpropagation and its wide use. (2) Turn on the computing-power lane and compare its slope before and after 2010. (3) Open the Minsky and Papert card and switch between lore and record.
- What to notice: the ideas lane is dense long before 2012; the computing-power lane is what changes.
- Build: event data in a JSON file, drawn with D3; large.

**Changes made while building (2026-10-16).**

- The event data (59 events, every date and statement checked against a source on 2026-10-16) is in `docs/data/timeline.json`, with a source URL for each event and a second source where one was used. Events were added beyond the draft list (Moore's law, CUDA, GPUs for deep nets, TPU, MNIST, ALPAC, Mansfield Amendment, Strategic Computing, Google and DeepMind, Microsoft and OpenAI, the AI Safety Summit, the EU AI Act, and others) so that the computing and funding lanes are not empty.
- The 1958 New York Times quote was checked: the story ran on July 8, 1958, page 25, and attributed the expectation to the Navy (Ananthaswamy, *Why Machines Learn*, 2024). The perceptron card shows it as lore, with the record that the demonstration was an IBM 704 learning to tell cards marked left from right after 50 trials (Cornell, 2019).
- Lore and record cards: perceptron (1958), Dartmouth (the name appears in the August 31, 1955 proposal), Minsky and Papert (Olazaran 1993 and 1996), Deep Blue (search and a programmer-written evaluation function), and deep belief nets (2006). The AlphaGo "move 37" item is stated as record only (DeepMind's 1 in 10,000 figure); no contested lore was found to set against it.
- The draft's "2024 o1, the first widely released reasoning model" is kept; o1-preview was released September 12, 2024. The 2025 row names Claude Code (February 24, 2025) and OpenAI Codex (May 16, 2025).
- The second winter is shaded 1987 to 1993; the sidenote says that some accounts extend it to 2000 (Wikipedia's AI winter article uses 1987 to 2000).
- The computing lane can show training compute (Epoch AI data in `docs/data/compute.json`: every pre-2010 model in the dataset, 62 points, plus the 34 notable models of page 1.5) with fitted lines; task 2 quotes the fitted rates (1.5 times per year before 2010, 4.8 after) and Sevilla et al. (2022) for the full dataset (about 20 and 6 months).
- Task 1 is supported by a measurement in the tool: each card states how many years it comes after the card opened before.
- The check question asks what AlexNet had that LeCun's 1989 network lacked, because the draft question is answered in the page text. A second check asks which statement fits the timeline.
- The static figure is vertical, so its 12 labels fit at phone width; it sits after the tasks.
- On phones the timeline keeps its full height and fits the width; zoom, year-range buttons, and Previous and Next event buttons replace horizontal scrolling. Events with no room for a label sit as dots on the lane's top rule.
