## 1.2 Perceptrons and the first winter (1943 to 1980)

**Aim.** The reader can say what a perceptron computes (a linear threshold function), has watched the perceptron learning rule succeed on separable data and fail on XOR, and can say why that limit was real but was not the whole cause of the first winter.

**Text (draft).**

> A perceptron takes a list of numbers, multiplies each by a weight, and adds them up. It outputs 1 if the sum exceeds a threshold and 0 otherwise. Geometrically, a perceptron draws a straight line (in more dimensions, a flat plane called a hyperplane) and labels everything on one side 1.
>
> Rosenblatt's learning rule adjusts the weights each time the perceptron gets an example wrong. If some line separates the two classes, the rule finds one in a finite number of steps. This result is the perceptron convergence theorem.
>
> XOR is the function that outputs 1 when exactly one of its two inputs is 1. No line separates XOR's two 1-points from its two 0-points, so no perceptron computes XOR. Minsky and Papert's 1969 book *Perceptrons* proved results of this kind for broader classes of functions. A network with a hidden layer between input and output can compute XOR, but in 1969 there was no practical method for training hidden layers.
>
> AI funding fell sharply in the mid-1970s. The book is often blamed. The documented causes also include the UK's 1973 Lighthill report, which judged that AI had not delivered on its promises, and new US rules tying military research funding to direct military applications.

**Image.** The four XOR points on the unit square, with three candidate lines, each misclassifying at least one point.

**Interactive: "Perceptron playground"** (shared with 2.2).

- Display: points in the plane, the perceptron's line, misclassified points ringed, and a plot of errors per pass through the data.
- Controls: datasets (AND, OR, XOR, random separable, random non-separable); drag the line by hand, or press Step or Run to apply the learning rule; learning-rate slider; a switch that adds a hidden layer of two perceptrons whose weights the reader sets by hand.
- Try: (1) Separate AND by dragging the line. (2) Run the learning rule on OR and count the steps. (3) Run it on XOR and watch it cycle. (4) Switch on the hidden layer and set weights by hand to compute XOR.
- What to notice: on XOR the error count never reaches zero. With the hidden layer a solution exists, but the perceptron rule has no way to find it.
- Build: live; small.

**Check.** Can a single perceptron compute "x1 AND NOT x2"? Answer: yes. Its only 1-point, (1, 0), can be cut off from the other three points by a line.

**Links.** Forward to 2.2 (the neuron) and 2.3 (why nonlinearity matters). References: Rosenblatt (1958); Minsky and Papert (1969); Olazaran (1996), "A sociological study of the official history of the perceptrons controversy".
