## 1.6 Assistants and reasoning models (2022 to 2025)

**Aim.** The reader knows the three steps after 2022: fine-tuning with human feedback (ChatGPT), chain-of-thought prompting, and models trained with reinforcement learning to reason at length before answering.

**Text (draft).**

> A model trained only to predict internet text continues documents. Given a question, it may answer it, or it may write more questions, because both happen in its training data. InstructGPT (2022) fine-tuned GPT-3 on example answers and then on human rankings of its answers, a method called reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF (Section 4.2). Labelers preferred the answers of a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT model to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3. ChatGPT, released in November 2022, used the same method in a chat interface.
>
> In the same year, Wei et al. showed that prompting a model with worked examples that write out intermediate steps, called chain-of-thought prompting, improved its accuracy on arithmetic and logic problems. Writing out steps gives the model more computation per answer (Section 4.4).
>
> Starting with OpenAI's o1 (September 2024) and DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025), labs trained models with reinforcement learning on problems whose answers can be checked automatically, such as math and coding problems. These reasoning models learned to write long chains of thought, to check their work, and to backtrack. In 2025 attention moved to agents: models that carry out tasks over many steps using tools.

**Image.** One prompt ("What is the capital of Australia?") with three outputs: a base model that continues with more quiz questions, a chat model that answers, and a reasoning model that answers after a short chain of thought.

**Interactive: "Same prompt, three models".**

- Display: a prompt and three output panels.
- Controls: a menu of 8 prompts, chosen to separate the models (a question, an instruction, a math word problem, a question with a false premise).
- Data: real outputs generated once from an open-weight model family that publishes a base model, an instruction-tuned version, and a reasoning version. Outputs must be generated, not written by hand.
- Try: (1) Find a prompt where the base model continues the document instead of answering. (2) On the word problem, find where the reasoning model catches its own error.
- What to notice: the three models share most of their weights and knowledge; post-training changed what they do with it.
- Build: precomputed; small.

**Check.** Does RLHF mainly add knowledge to a model? Answer: no. Evidence suggests most knowledge comes from pretraining and post-training mainly changes which behaviors the model produces (Zhou et al. 2023, the LIMA paper).

**Links.** Forward to 4.1 to 4.4. References: Ouyang et al. (2022); Wei et al. (2022); DeepSeek-AI (2025).

**Changes made while building (2026-10-16).**

- Model family: Qwen3-0.6B-Base (base) and Qwen3-0.6B with thinking off (chat) and on (reasoning). Qwen3 publishes a base model and one post-trained model that runs in both modes, so the chat and reasoning columns share parameters. Qwen3-1.7B was tried first, but on the shared 7 GB machine its weights kept being paged out and generation fell to about 0.25 tokens per second. The page says that the small Qwen3 models learned to reason by distillation from larger Qwen3 models, not through their own reinforcement learning (Qwen3 technical report, section 4.5).
- The outcomes differ from the spec's expectations, and the tasks report them as measured. The base model continued the document on only one of eight prompts (Einstein), because Qwen3's pretraining text includes trillions of generated question-answer and instruction tokens (verified in the report); task 1 asks the reader to predict how many. No reasoning output "catches its own error" cleanly; instead task 2 (letter count: chat says 1, reasoning counts 3) and task 3 (bat and ball: the reasoning model finds $0.05, then talks itself into doubt and hits the 3,000-token limit with no answer) show what checking does and does not do in a 0.6B model. Task 4 shows all three models accepting both false premises.
- Added after the tasks: the measured size of the parameter change from base to post-trained model (6.3% overall, `tools/1-6/weight_diff.py`), and the LIMA finding.
- The static figure uses the letter-count prompt rather than the capital of Australia, since all three models answered the capital question the same way.
- Added a second check question (why the reasoning mode counted correctly with the same parameters). Outputs are rendered from their Markdown with a "exactly as written" switch; model outputs are stored verbatim, so they can contain characters the site's prose avoids (an em dash appears in one generated haiku).
