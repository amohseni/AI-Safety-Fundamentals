## 1.4 Deep learning (2006 to 2016)

**Aim.** The reader can name the ingredients behind AlexNet's 2012 result (a large labeled dataset, GPU training, the ReLU activation, dropout, and an existing architecture made larger) and the main advances that followed through AlphaGo.

**Text (draft).**

> In 2012 a convolutional network called AlexNet won the ImageNet image-classification challenge with a top-5 error of 15.3% (16.4% for its version trained without extra data). The next-best entry, which did not use a neural network, had 26.2%. The architecture was not new: it was a larger version of LeCun's convolutional network. What was new was the combination of 1.2 million labeled training images, training on two consumer GPUs, the ReLU activation function, which lessens the vanishing gradient problem, and dropout, a method that reduces overfitting.
>
> A convolutional network applies the same small learned filter at every position of an image. Early layers learn filters for edges and colors; later layers combine them into textures, parts, and objects.
>
> Within a few years neural networks led in image recognition, speech recognition, and machine translation. Several inventions from these years are still in use: word embeddings (word2vec, 2013), the Adam optimizer (2014), attention for translation (2014), and residual connections (ResNet, 2015), which made it possible to train networks with more than 100 layers. In 2016 DeepMind's AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol at Go, four games to one, by combining neural networks trained on human games and on self-play with tree search.

**Image.** Bar chart of the winning ImageNet top-5 error by year, 2010 to 2017, with the estimated human error (about 5%) as a line and the 2012 bar labeled AlexNet.

**Interactive: "What a filter sees".**

- Display: an image, a 3 by 3 filter as editable numbers, and the filtered output (the feature map).
- Controls: preset filters (vertical edge, horizontal edge, blur, sharpen); AlexNet's 96 learned first-layer filters, shown as a grid the reader can click to apply; a second tab with the ImageNet chart, where each bar opens the winning entry and its main change.
- Try: (1) Apply the vertical-edge filter and predict the output before you look. (2) Find AlexNet filters that resemble the hand-made edge filters. (3) Edit a filter by hand to detect diagonal edges.
- What to notice: the network learned edge detectors that engineers used to design by hand.
- Build: live filters, AlexNet filter weights shipped as data; small.

**Check.** Was AlexNet's architecture new in 2012? Answer: no. It scaled up LeCun's design; the new parts were the data, GPU training, ReLU, and dropout.

**Links.** Back to 1.3; forward to 2.3 (ReLU) and 2.8 (overfitting). References: Krizhevsky, Sutskever, and Hinton (2012); Russakovsky et al. (2015), the ImageNet challenge paper; Silver et al. (2016).

**Review (2026-10-16).**

- The first check asked which of three things was "not new in AlexNet", with "Training a large network on GPUs" as a wrong answer. GPU training of networks was not new in 2012 (Raina et al. 2009; Ciresan et al. 2011), and ImageNet had existed since 2009, so the question had more than one defensible answer. It now asks which of the three LeCun's 1989 network already had (answer: a convolutional architecture trained with backpropagation).
- Dropout is described as switching off a random half of the units "in some layers" (AlexNet used it in the first two fully connected layers).
- ImageNet chart: the "0%" tick label was cut off at the left edge; the first and last tick labels are now anchored inside. The dashed human-error line no longer crosses the row labels.
