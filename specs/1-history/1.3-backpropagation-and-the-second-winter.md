## 1.3 Backpropagation and the second winter (1980 to 2005)

**Aim.** The reader knows that backpropagation made multilayer networks trainable in principle, that expert systems drove the 1980s boom and its collapse, and that deep networks still trained poorly because the training signal shrinks as it passes back through many sigmoid layers.

**Text (draft).**

> Backpropagation computes how much each weight in a multilayer network contributed to the error, so that all the weights can be adjusted at once. Section 2.7 explains how. The method was derived by Linnainmaa in 1970, proposed for neural networks by Werbos in 1974, and became widely known through a 1986 paper by Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams.
>
> The AI boom of the 1980s was mostly about expert systems: programs built from if-then rules written down by interviewing human experts. Expert systems worked in narrow settings and were expensive to build and maintain. The market for specialized AI computers collapsed in 1987, and a second winter followed.
>
> Neural networks kept improving on a smaller scale. LeCun's convolutional networks read handwritten zip codes by 1989, and the LSTM (1997) handled sequences. But networks with many layers trained poorly. Hochreiter identified one reason in 1991. When the error signal is passed backward through many sigmoid layers, it is multiplied by a small number at each layer, so it shrinks toward zero and the early layers barely learn. This is the vanishing gradient problem.

**Check.** Why does the sigmoid make gradients shrink? Answer: its derivative is at most 0.25, and backpropagation multiplies by one such factor per layer.
