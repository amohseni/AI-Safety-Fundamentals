## 4.4 Chain of thought and reasoning models

**Aim.** The reader understands why writing out intermediate steps lets a model of fixed depth do more computation, how reinforcement learning on checkable problems produced long reasoning, and why the faithfulness of written reasoning is an open safety question.

**Text (draft).**

> A transformer does a fixed amount of computation per token: one pass through its layers. A problem that needs more sequential steps than one pass provides cannot be solved in a single token. Writing out intermediate steps gets around this limit. Each written token is fed back in as input, so the text serves as a scratchpad, and the total computation grows with the number of tokens written. Theoretical work supports this: with enough intermediate steps, transformers can solve problems that a single pass provably cannot (Feng et al. 2023; Merrill and Sabharwal 2024).
>
> Chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al. 2022) drew out this behavior with worked examples in the prompt. Reasoning models are trained for it. They write a long chain of thought and then an answer, and reinforcement learning rewards correct final answers on problems that can be checked automatically. The DeepSeek-R1 report (2025) found that behaviors such as rechecking and backtracking arose from this training without being demonstrated.
>
> Whether the written reasoning reflects the computation that actually produced the answer is an open question. Experiments find cases where a model's stated reasons leave out factors that changed its answer (Turpin et al. 2023). Reading the chain of thought is currently one of the main ways to monitor models (Section 8.6), so its faithfulness matters.
