## 5.1 Universal approximation

**Aim.** The reader can state the universal approximation theorem, sees why it is true by building approximations out of ReLU units, and can say what the theorem does not establish.

**Text (draft).**

> The universal approximation theorem (Cybenko 1989; Hornik 1991; Leshno et al. 1993) says: a network with one hidden layer and any activation function that is not a polynomial can approximate any continuous function on a bounded region as closely as desired, given enough hidden units.
>
> For ReLU the idea is easy to see. Two ReLU units combine into a ramp, and two ramps make a bump. Enough bumps of the right heights trace out any continuous curve.
>
> The theorem is an existence result. It does not say how many units are needed (the number can grow exponentially with the input's dimension), whether gradient descent will find the right weights, or whether the network will generalize from finite data. Its lesson is that the ability to represent a function is rarely the bottleneck. Depth also matters: some functions that a deep network computes with few units require exponentially many units in a shallow one (Telgarsky 2016; Eldan and Shamir 2016).

**Image.** A target curve and its approximations with 4, 8, and 32 ReLU units, with each unit's contribution drawn faintly beneath.

**Interactive: "Build any function".**

- Mode 1, by hand: the reader draws a target curve, then adds ReLU units one at a time, dragging each unit's kink position, slope, and sign; the sum is drawn over the target.
- Mode 2, fit: choose the number of units and fit them automatically; plot the error against the number of units.
- Mode 3, depth: a sawtooth target; compare a one-hidden-layer and a two-hidden-layer network with the same total number of units.
- Try: (1) Build a bump from three units by hand. (2) Draw a jagged curve and find how many units bring the error below 0.01. (3) In mode 3, compare the two networks on the sawtooth.
- What to notice: any curve can be built from enough simple pieces, and depth can make the number of pieces needed much smaller.
- Build: live; medium.

**Check.** Does the theorem imply that a network can learn any function from data? Answer: no. It says a good approximation exists; it says nothing about whether training finds it or whether finite data pins it down.

**Links.** Back to 2.3; forward to 5.3. References: Cybenko (1989); Hornik (1991); Leshno et al. (1993); Telgarsky (2016).

**Changes made while building (2026-10-16).**

- The theorem is stated for continuous non-polynomial activation functions (Leshno et al. require locally bounded, piecewise continuous, non-polynomial; continuity is the simpler sufficient condition).
- Mode 2 fits with evenly spaced kinks and least squares on the slopes and constant (stated on the page), so the error-against-units curve is deterministic. Jagged target: error first below 0.01 at 42 units and stays below up to 128 (`tools/test-page-5-1.mjs`).
- Mode 3 compares k hidden layers of 2 units (Telgarsky's tent map composed k times) with one hidden layer of the same 2k units, for k = 1 to 6, instead of only one versus two hidden layers: the gap only appears from k = 3 (with 2 layers, 4 evenly spaced shallow units already match the sawtooth exactly). Tested: deep net exact with 2^k pieces; shallow error 0.29 (even kinks) and 0.25 (best of 2,000 random kink sets) at k = 4; one hidden layer needs 2^k - 1 units.
- The "two ramps make a bump" sentence moved into task 1's notice, since task 1 asks how many units a bump needs. The checks were changed so they require reasoning: a piece count for a 20-unit network, and whether a failed fit contradicts the theorem.
- Drawing a target is pointer-only; keyboard users have the three preset targets.
