## 5.3 Double descent

**Aim.** The reader sees that as model size grows, test error can fall, rise to a peak at the size where the model can just fit every training example, and then fall again; and understands why the peak occurs there.

**Text (draft).**

> Section 2.8 showed the classical U-shaped curve. If the model keeps growing past the size at which it can fit every training example exactly, called the interpolation threshold, test error often falls again, sometimes below the bottom of the U. This is double descent (Belkin et al. 2019; Nakkiran et al. 2019).
>
> The peak occurs at the threshold because a model with just enough capacity to fit the data has essentially one way to do it, and that one fit must bend sharply to pass through every noisy point. A model with many more parameters than data points has many exact fits, and gradient descent started from small weights tends to find a smooth one; in linear models it finds the fit with the smallest weights (the minimum-norm solution).
>
> Double descent also appears as training time grows and as the dataset grows, and it is strongest when the labels are noisy. It is one reason the classical advice to keep models small does not carry over to large networks.

**Interactive: "Through the threshold".**

- Display: 40 noisy training points from a smooth curve and the fitted curve; train and test error against the number of features p, with the current p marked.
- Model: regression on p random ReLU features, fitted by minimum-norm least squares.
- Controls: p from 1 to 1,000 (log slider); label noise; strength of ridge regularization; resample the features.
- Try: (1) Set p = 40 and look at the fitted curve. (2) Move to p = 400. (3) Add a little ridge regularization and watch the peak disappear.
- What to notice: the peak sits at p equal to the number of data points, and it comes from forcing the fit through the noise.
- Build: live; medium.

**Check.** Why does regularization remove the peak? Answer: it stops the fit from passing exactly through every noisy point at the threshold.

**Changes made while building (2026-10-16).**

- Features are ReLU units on an arc encoding of x, ReLU(w . (cos 0.75 pi x, sin 0.75 pi x) + b), redrawn unless their kink lies among the 40 inputs. With plain one-input ReLU features, a feature's values on the data depend only on which gap between data points holds its kink, so the rank of the feature matrix reaches 40 only after hundreds of features and the peak does not sit at p = n. The page explains this in a sidenote.
- Training inputs are a jittered grid (fixed), so no two points nearly coincide; noisy labels on near-duplicate inputs otherwise force spikes at every p.
- Tested over 20 feature draws (`tools/test-page-5-3.mjs`): the peak falls between p = 36 and 44 in 16 of 20; the geometric mean peaks at p = 42; p = 400 beats p = 40 in every draw; ridge 0.001 caps the error for p >= 20 at 0.040; with no noise the peak disappears. In this toy the second descent (about 0.09) does not go below the best small model (about 0.03); the page says so.
- The readout reports the p at which the current draw first interpolates. Test error is measured against the true curve, not noisy test labels.
- The p slider is logarithmic, with buttons for p = 10, 40, 400. Ridge strengths are a segmented control (0 to 0.1).
