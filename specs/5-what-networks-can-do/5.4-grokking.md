## 5.4 Grokking

**Aim.** The reader watches a network first memorize its training data and, much later, suddenly generalize, and connects the jump to weight decay and to the gradual formation of a simpler algorithm inside the network (setting up 6.7).

**Text (draft).**

> Power et al. (2022) trained small transformers on modular arithmetic, such as computing (a + b) mod 113, using a fraction of all possible pairs (a, b). Training accuracy reached 100% quickly. Test accuracy stayed near chance for thousands more steps and then rose to 100%. They called this delayed generalization grokking.
>
> Nanda et al. (2023) reverse-engineered such a network. Early in training it memorizes the training pairs. Meanwhile a second solution forms gradually: an algorithm that represents each number as rotations (sines and cosines at a few frequencies) and adds numbers using trigonometric identities. Weight decay, which penalizes large weights, favors this algorithm, because it needs smaller weights than a memorized table does. Test accuracy jumps when the memorized solution is finally cleaned away.
>
> Grokking shows that a network's behavior on its training data does not reveal which algorithm it uses. Two networks with identical training accuracy can be running different computations. Section 6.7 opens this network and reads its algorithm.

**Image.** Train and test accuracy against training step on a log axis: training accuracy at 100% early, test accuracy jumping late.

**Interactive: "Grok it yourself".**

- Display: train and test accuracy curves with a time cursor; a 113 by 113 grid of all (a, b) pairs, colored by whether the model answers correctly at the current time and by whether the pair was in the training set; a panel showing how concentrated the embedding is on a few frequencies.
- Controls: weight decay; fraction of pairs used for training; scrub through training time.
- Data: checkpoints from training runs done in advance for a grid of weight-decay values and training fractions.
- Try: (1) Set weight decay to 0: does the network grok? (2) Compare training on 30% and 70% of pairs. (3) Scrub to just before the jump and look at the frequency panel.
- What to notice: the frequency structure forms before test accuracy jumps.
- Build: precomputed checkpoints (trained in Python); large.

**Check.** Just before the jump, is the network only memorizing? Answer: no. The generalizing algorithm is already partly built, but the memorized solution still dominates the output.

**Links.** Back to 2.8, 5.3; forward to 5.5, 6.7. References: Power et al. (2022); Nanda et al. (2023), "Progress measures for grokking via mechanistic interpretability".

**Changes made while building (2026-10-16).**

- The runs use p = 59, not 113, with Nanda et al.'s model and training otherwise unchanged. At p = 113 a step took 0.1 seconds or more on the build machine, too slow for the nine-setting grid; at p = 59 the model still groks. The page says so in a sidenote. The grid of pairs is 59 by 59.
- The text no longer says Power et al. used (a + b) mod 113. They used tables of binary operations modulo 97; the numbers quoted (training accuracy near 100% within 1,000 steps, held-out accuracy rising only after about 1,000 times as many) are from their division run with half the pairs, as the sidenote says.
- Nanda et al.'s account (frequencies, phases, the role of weight decay) moved below the tasks, so it does not answer them in advance. It is followed by a measurement from this site's run: the restricted accuracy (Nanda et al.'s restricted loss, scored as accuracy) is 78% at step 3,600, when the full model's test accuracy is 19%. This supports the check answer with a number the reader can trace.
- Task 1 reports what the runs show: without weight decay, the model never generalizes within 20,000 steps, and nearly all the test pairs it answers correctly are reverses of training pairs. That finding was not in the spec; it was measured (`tools/test-page-5-4.mjs`).
- Task 3 names a step (3,600) instead of "just before the jump", because test accuracy in this run rises gradually from about 7% to 25% between steps 1,000 and 4,000 before the steep rise.
- The frequency panel is summarized by one number drawn on the accuracy chart: the share of the embedding in its five largest frequencies. The static figure adds the restricted accuracy as a dashed line.
- Training time is shown on a log scale. Checkpoints are 40 log-spaced steps up to 1,000, then every 200 steps.
- A second check question asks whether 100% training accuracy shows that a model has learned the rule.
- Data and pipeline: `tools/modadd/` (README there), data in `docs/data/modadd/` (1.3 MB).
