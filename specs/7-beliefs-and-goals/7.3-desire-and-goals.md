## 7.3 Desire and goals

**Aim.** The reader can state the book's criteria for desire (coherence, intelligibility, and use, plus systematicity for representationalists), knows the behavioral sign of goal-directedness (many means toward few ends across environments), and can design the interventions that give internal evidence for a desire: ablation, goal substitution, and joint tests of belief and desire.

**Text (draft).**

> Desire differs from belief in its direction of fit: a belief aims to match the world, a desire aims to make the world match it. So desire has no truth criterion, and the book puts intelligibility in its place: an attributed desire should have content we can grasp and should be specific enough to predict behavior (§5.1, §5.3). **Coherence** requires that means fit ends and that preferences be transitive, and it fits the averaging rule of 7.1 (§5.2). **Use** requires that the desire drive behavior and that the ends stay stable: an agent that drops a coding task to write poetry, with no change in its instructions, gives evidence against a stable desire (§5.4). Representationalists add **systematicity** (§5.5). A change in desire that no change in belief explains is a warning sign (§5.6).
>
> The behavioral sign of desire, following Goldstein and Lederman, is a system that "takes a wide range of means to rationally promote a small number of ends across a range of environments" (§5.4). A thermostat fails this test: it has one means. Agentic systems, which act over many steps with tools, supply far more evidence of this kind than chat transcripts do (§3.5).
>
> Internal evidence comes from interventions. Removing a goal representation should disrupt goal-directed behavior. Substituting it is more informative: swap the representation of one goal for another and check whether the means change sensibly, as when a chess model's goal of checkmate is replaced by building a pawn triangle (§5.4.2). Desire cannot be tested in isolation: changing a credence (the bug is in the parser, not the renderer) should change preferences the way the averaging rule predicts (§5.2.2). The book's current assessment is that beliefs have some representational backing, while desires are interpretable but not yet shown to be represented (§5.8).

**Image.** Two panels: a thermostat (one means, one end) and a coding agent (many means toward one end across several environments). Below them, the goal-substitution test as a before-and-after diagram.

**Interactive: "Swap the goal".**

- Display: a gridworld agent run by a small policy network trained in advance; the goal location decoded from its activations; its path; a count of the distinct routes it uses to reach its goal across environments, next to the same count for a thermostat-like controller.
- Controls: environment variations (walls, doors, keys); remove the goal representation; substitute another goal's representation; change the agent's decoded belief about which door is open.
- Try: (1) Compare the agent and the thermostat on the means count. (2) Predict what removing the goal representation will do, then remove it. (3) Substitute a new goal and check whether the route changes sensibly. (4) Change the belief about the door and predict the new route before running it.
- What to notice: a desire attribution gains support when changing the goal representation changes the means sensibly, and when changing beliefs changes preferences as expected-desire maximization predicts.
- Build: policy trained in advance, run live in the browser; medium to large.

**Check.** Removing a direction stops goal-directed behavior. Why is goal substitution stronger evidence than removal? Answer: removal shows the direction is needed for the behavior; substitution shows it carries the content of the goal, because the means shift to serve the new goal.

**Changes made while building (2026-10-16).**

- The network is trained in advance by imitation, not reinforcement learning: on 1.6 million random states it learned to pick a move on a shortest route to the goal, as computed by a planner (`tools/s73`). It has 316 inputs (walls, doors, key, agent, goal, key in hand), two ReLU layers of 128, and 4 outputs; the parameters, subspaces, and probes ship in `docs/data/7-3/policy.json` (377 KB) and run live in the browser. In 1,000 new random environments it reached its goal every time, by a shortest route in 999. The page says it is a toy model and that its goal is marked in its input, as the cheese is in Mini et al.'s maze. "Policy network" is avoided because the Fixed terms reserve "policy" for a model trained with reinforcement learning.
- The interventions act on the first layer's activations at every step. The goal subspace (32 directions) and the door subspace (12 directions) are the top principal directions of the change in those activations when only the goal, or only the doors, are changed (40,000 held-out states). Subspaces spanned by the per-goal mean activations were tried first and did not work: swapping along them reached the new goal in at most 64% of runs even at 48 directions. Removal replaces the goal-subspace component with its mean; substitution and the door belief replace the component with the one computed for the counterfactual goal or doors. Every number on the page comes from `tools/test-page-7-3.mjs`, including controls with random subspaces of the same sizes (removal leaves 66% to 76% of goals reached, against 10% for the goal subspace; random swaps reach the new goal in 16% to 18%, against 93.5%).
- The "thermostat-like controller" is a direct controller that moves along its row toward the goal's column and then along that column, pushing against anything in its way. The means count is the number of distinct paths over six fixed environments (network: 6 of 6 reached by 6 paths; direct controller: 2 of 6 by 1 path).
- The door-belief control sets a belief that one door is open and the other closed. It offers no intermediate credences: the network was never trained under uncertainty about the doors, so the averaging rule makes no testable prediction for it at intermediate values.
