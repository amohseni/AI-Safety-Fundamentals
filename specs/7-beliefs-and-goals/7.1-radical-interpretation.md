## 7.1 Radical interpretation

**Aim.** The reader can state the interpreter's task (from the physical facts about a system, find beliefs, desires, and the propositions they are about, such that the system's behavior comes out as maximizing expected desire), knows the two views of what makes such an attribution correct, and sees why behavior alone cannot settle it.

**Text (draft).**

> Lewis posed the problem of radical interpretation about an imagined person, Karl: given all the physical facts about Karl, solve for his beliefs, desires, and meanings. Herrmann and Levinstein put an AI system in Karl's place. The evidence comes in two kinds: internal facts (the weights and the architecture) and behavioral facts (what the system does) (§1.1).
>
> The target is an agential description with three parts: a belief function P, which gives subjective probabilities; a desire function D, which says how much the agent wishes each proposition were true; and an algebra of propositions over which both are defined. The description is correct only if the system's behavior can be rationalized as maximizing expected desire, with the expectation taken under P (§1.2). Candidate descriptions are compared by how well they predict behavior and by how rational the agent then appears (§1.3).
>
> Two views say what makes an attribution correct. On interpretationism, a system has certain beliefs and desires if attributing them yields the best theory of the system: the most accurate, powerful, and tractable one. On representationalism, it has them if it contains internal representations that play the right functional roles. The book treats the two as allies for this project (§3.1).
>
> Behavior alone leaves the answer open. In the Jeffrey-Bolker decision theory the book uses, the same preferences are represented by many different belief-desire pairs (§3.4.4). Inverse reinforcement learning meets the same problem: many reward functions make the same behavior optimal (Ng and Russell 2000). Internal evidence can break the tie, for example a truth probe that narrows which belief functions are admissible. Lewis and Davidson could not look inside their subjects; we can (§6.6).

**Formula.** Desirability is an average over the ways a proposition can be true (for any partition S₁, ..., Sₙ), and preferences fix P and D only up to the transformation on the right (with ad − bc > 0 and cD + d > 0):

```latex
D(A) = \sum_i D(A \wedge S_i)\, P(S_i \mid A) \qquad P'(A) = P(A)\,\big(cD(A) + d\big), \quad D'(A) = \frac{aD(A) + b}{cD(A) + d}
```

**Image.** The book's triangle (Fig. 1): physical facts, behavioral evidence, and the agential description at the corners; the interpreter's job is to make the three cohere.

**Interactive: "One behavior, many minds".**

- Scenario: a coding agent chooses which file to edit. The states are where the bug is (parser, renderer, elsewhere).
- Display: the agent's credences P over the states; its desirabilities D for each action-and-state combination; the desirability of each action, computed by averaging; the action it chooses.
- Controls: sliders for P and D; a transformation panel with a, b, c, d that turns the current pair into another pair; a "probe reading" switch that fixes P to what a truth probe reports.
- Try: (1) Set P and D so the agent edits the parser. (2) Predict whether a transformation will change its choice, then apply one: P and D change, the choice does not. (3) Fix P with the probe reading and see what freedom remains (only rescaling D and shifting its zero point).
- What to notice: behavior fixes a family of belief-desire pairs; evidence about one component from inside the system shrinks the family.
- Build: live; medium.

**Check.** Why can't behavior alone settle what an agent believes? Answer: many belief-desire pairs produce exactly the same preferences, so behavior cannot choose among them; extra evidence, such as an internal reading of the belief function, is needed.
