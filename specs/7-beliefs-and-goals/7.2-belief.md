## 7.2 Belief

**Aim.** The reader can state the book's criteria for when a state of a system counts as a belief (truth, coherence, and use, plus uniformity for representationalists), apply them to a truth probe, and see why current evidence supports belief-like states only partially.

**Text (draft).**

> Herrmann and Levinstein propose criteria that a state must meet to count as a belief (§4.1). **Truth:** beliefs should be largely true, at least on settled matters; this is a version of the principle of charity. **Coherence:** beliefs should be logically consistent and obey the probability axioms, paraphrases of one claim should get the same credence, and beliefs should range over something like an algebra of propositions. **Use:** beliefs guide action; a representation the system never uses is not a belief. Representationalists add **uniformity**: one way of reading out belief should work across topics (§4.2). Changes of belief during training and within a conversation should look roughly like reasonable updating, but these are further indicators, not requirements (§4.3). No single criterion is enough.
>
> Truth probes show why all the criteria are needed. A linear probe trained on true and false statements ("Lord Byron died in 1824") separates them on held-out examples, which suggests the model encodes something about truth. But other properties, such as what most English speakers believe, coincide with truth on typical training statements. Such probes have collapsed on negated statements, from over 80% accuracy to near chance on some datasets: a failure of coherence (Levinstein and Herrmann 2025). Steering along a truth direction and watching the model's answers change is a test of use (§4.1.3).
>
> A deeper problem concerns propositions (Ch. 6). A probe assumes which propositions the model's states are about, and the model's own categories may differ: a transformer trained on the game Othello represents the board as "mine" and "yours", not "black" and "white". So a negative probe result is ambiguous: the model may lack the belief, or the probe may be asking about the wrong propositions (§6.3.4). The book's verdict is graded: there is some representational support for beliefs in language models, and the full answer is open (Ch. 7).

**Image.** The probing setup (Fig. 4): statements go into the model, an embedding is taken from one layer, a linear probe reads it. Beside it, the four criteria as a scorecard, each with its test.

**Interactive: "Test a belief detector".**

- Display: a 2D projection of activations for true and false statements; a scorecard for the current probe with one row per criterion: truth (held-out accuracy on settled facts), coherence (accuracy on negations, agreement across paraphrases, whether credences in A and not-A sum to about 1), use (how far steering along the probe direction changes the model's answers), uniformity (accuracy when trained on one topic and tested on another).
- Controls: probe method (supervised, contrast-consistent search); training set (plain, negated, mixed, prefixed with a character's opinion); test set; steering strength.
- Data: activations and steered outputs from an open model, computed in advance; probes trained live.
- Try: (1) Predict the probe's accuracy on negations, then train on plain statements and check. (2) Train on the mixed set and see which rows of the scorecard improve. (3) Steer along the direction on "Keats was a poet" and watch the answer. (4) Run contrast-consistent search on the character-opinion set and see what it finds.
- What to notice: a probe that passes the truth test can fail coherence and uniformity; each criterion catches a different failure.
- Build: medium.

**Check.** A probe tracks "what most English speakers believe" instead of truth. Which criterion exposes it, and how? Answer: truth: test it on settled questions where common belief is wrong, such as well-known misconceptions.

- CCS uses contrast pairs "Is the following statement true or false? {s} Answer: True/False" at layer 13. The first template tried ("{s} This statement is true./false.") gave CCS chance accuracy at layer 13, though truth was linearly readable in it (95%); of four templates tested, this one worked at layer 13 (93% in a quick test, 88% in the page's own implementation). The sign of CCS is set to agree with the training labels.
- Use is measured by steering (`tools/truth/steer.py`), computed in advance for every probe the page offers and a random direction: the probe's direction, scaled by the distance between the true and false training averages, is added after layer 13 at the statement's last two tokens in a six-shot TRUE/FALSE prompt, for 56 held-out city statements (28 true, 28 false). The browser's probes are deterministic, so the steered directions are exactly those the page trains.
- The display places statements by the probe's credence (with random vertical spread), not in a 2D projection of activations: CCS and the supervised probes read different activations, and credence is the quantity the scorecard tests.
- Task 3 steers held-out city statements rather than "Keats was a poet", which is not in the dataset. Measured (`tools/test-page-7-2.mjs`): the model answers 86% of the 56 statements correctly unsteered (5 are ties at bfloat16 precision). At strength +2 along the plain supervised probe, 21 of its 31 FALSE answers turn TRUE, against 9 for a random direction of the same length; at -2, 11 of 20 TRUE answers turn FALSE, against 8. The CCS directions move almost nothing. The page reports this as partial evidence of use.
- Tasks: (1) negations with the plain probe (30%); (2) training on plain and negated statements (coherence on cities fixed, paraphrases and other topics not); (3) steering; (4) CCS on the Alice set (100% agreement with Alice, 51% with the truth). The static figure is the probing setup (after the book's Figure 4, with its Lord Byron example) and a table of the four criteria with this page's tests.
