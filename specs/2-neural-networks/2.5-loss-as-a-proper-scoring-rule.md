## 2.5 Loss as a proper scoring rule

**Aim.** The reader understands cross-entropy loss as the logarithmic scoring rule from forecasting, sees that it is strictly proper (a forecaster minimizes expected loss only by reporting their true probabilities), and draws the consequence: next-token training pushes a model toward calibrated probabilities. The reader also knows that pretraining is supervised learning, not reinforcement learning.

**Text (draft).**

> A language model outputs a probability for every possible next token. Training scores that output with the cross-entropy loss: minus the log of the probability the model gave to the token that actually came next. If the model gave the right token probability 0.9, the loss is 0.105. If it gave 0.01, the loss is 4.6.
>
> Cross-entropy is the logarithmic scoring rule from the study of forecasts. A scoring rule is strictly proper if a forecaster minimizes their expected loss only by reporting their true probabilities. The log rule is strictly proper, and so is the Brier score (squared error). Not every rule is. Under the linear rule, where the loss is one minus the probability given to the outcome, a forecaster does best by putting all their probability on the most likely outcome.
>
> Because the training loss is strictly proper, training to predict the next token pushes a model toward calibrated probabilities: among the cases where it says 70%, the event should happen about 70% of the time. The GPT-4 technical report found the pretrained model well calibrated on a multiple-choice benchmark, and less calibrated after training with human feedback (Section 4).
>
> Pretraining is not reinforcement learning. The targets come from the text itself, so it is supervised learning, often called self-supervised learning. Reinforcement learning enters later (Section 4.1).

**Formula.** Expected log loss when the true probability of an event is p and the report is q, minimized exactly at q = p:

```latex
\mathbb{E}[L(q)] = -\,p \log q \; - \; (1-p)\log(1-q)
```

**Image.** Expected loss as a function of the reported probability q when the true probability is 0.7, for the log, Brier, and linear rules. The log and Brier curves have their minimum at 0.7; the linear rule's minimum is at 1.

**Interactive: "The forecaster's game".** One of the first eight tools to build.

- Panel 1: a coin with hidden bias p. The reader reports q with a slider and sees the expected loss curve for the chosen rule (log, Brier, linear), with their report marked.
- Panel 2: 1,000 simulated flips; average realized loss for three fixed strategies (honest, overconfident, underconfident), updating as flips arrive.
- Panel 3, the language-model version: a five-token vocabulary with a true next-token distribution shown as bars. The reader drags the model's bars; the expected cross-entropy updates.
- Try: (1) Under the linear rule with p = 0.7, find the best report. (2) Switch to the log rule and repeat. (3) In panel 3, give zero probability to a token that sometimes occurs.
- What to notice: only proper rules reward honest reports, and the log rule punishes overconfidence without limit (zero probability on an outcome that occurs costs infinite loss).

**Check.** A model trained with cross-entropy says "70%" about many claims. If it is calibrated, how often are those claims true? Answer: about 70% of the time.
