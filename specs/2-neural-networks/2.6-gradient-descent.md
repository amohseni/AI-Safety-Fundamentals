## 2.6 Gradient descent

**Aim.** The reader understands gradient descent as repeatedly stepping downhill on the loss, and knows what the learning rate, minibatch noise (stochastic gradient descent), momentum, and Adam do.

**Text (draft).**

> The gradient of the loss is the vector of its partial derivatives with respect to every parameter. It points in the direction in which the loss increases fastest. Gradient descent repeatedly moves the parameters a small step in the opposite direction: θ ← θ − η∇L(θ). The step size η is called the learning rate. Too large a learning rate overshoots and can diverge; too small a learning rate makes progress very slowly.
>
> Computing the exact gradient requires going through the whole dataset. In practice each step uses a small random batch of examples, so the gradient is a noisy estimate, and the method is called stochastic gradient descent (SGD). The estimate is far cheaper and good enough. There is evidence that the noise steers training toward flat regions of the loss surface, which are thought to generalize better, though this claim is debated.
>
> Current training uses refinements. Momentum keeps a running average of recent gradients, so steps continue in directions that stay consistent. Adam also scales each parameter's step by a running estimate of the size of that parameter's gradients. Nearly all large models are trained with Adam or a close variant such as AdamW.

**Image.** A contour plot of a long, narrow valley with three paths from the same starting point: a small learning rate (slow), a good one, and a large one (zig-zagging out of the valley).

**Interactive: "Roll downhill".** One of the first eight tools to build.

- Display: a contour plot of a two-parameter loss surface with the optimizer's path, and a plot of loss against step.
- Controls: surface (round bowl, narrow valley, Rosenbrock's curved valley, two separate minima); click to set the start point; learning rate (log slider); noise level, standing in for batch size; optimizer (plain gradient descent, momentum, Adam); speed.
- Try: (1) On the narrow valley, find the largest learning rate that still converges. (2) Compare plain gradient descent and momentum on the same valley. (3) On the two-minimum surface, add noise and see which minimum you end in across ten runs.
- What to notice: the shape of the surface decides how hard the optimization is, and momentum and Adam help most on narrow valleys.
- Build: live; medium.

**Check.** Why not compute the exact gradient at every step? Answer: it requires a pass through the whole dataset; a minibatch estimate costs a tiny fraction of that and works.
