## 2.7 Backpropagation

**Aim.** The reader understands backpropagation as the chain rule applied to the network's computation graph, worked from the output back to the inputs, and knows that it costs only a small multiple of one forward pass.

**Text (draft).**

> Gradient descent needs the derivative of the loss with respect to every parameter. A network is a composition of simple operations, so the chain rule gives each derivative as a product of local derivatives along the paths from that parameter to the loss.
>
> Backpropagation organizes this computation. First run the network forward and store every intermediate value. Then work backward from the loss. Each operation receives the derivative of the loss with respect to its output, multiplies it by its own local derivative, and passes the result to its inputs. Where two paths meet, their contributions add. Each intermediate derivative is computed once and reused by everything upstream of it.
>
> As a result, the gradient with respect to all parameters costs only a few times as much as one forward pass, whether the network has a thousand parameters or a trillion. This fact is what makes training large networks possible. Libraries such as PyTorch and JAX do it automatically. The general technique is called reverse-mode automatic differentiation.

**Check.** Why work backward rather than forward? Answer: there is one output (the loss) and many parameters. One backward pass gives the derivatives for all parameters; working forward would take one pass per parameter.

**Changes made while building (2026-10-16).**

- The nudge test changes the parameter by 0.001 up and down and divides the change in loss by 0.002 (a central difference).
