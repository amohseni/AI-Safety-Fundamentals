## 3.8 Scaling laws

**Aim.** The reader knows that loss falls predictably, as a power law, in parameters, data, and compute; how the Chinchilla result sets the compute-optimal balance; and what scaling laws do not predict.

**Text (draft).**

> Kaplan et al. (2020) trained many language models of different sizes and found that test loss falls as a power law in the number of parameters N, the number of training tokens D, and the training compute C, across many orders of magnitude, as long as the other two are not the bottleneck. On a log-log plot each relation is a straight line.
>
> Training compute is roughly C ≈ 6ND floating-point operations. For a fixed budget, there is a best balance between model size and data. Hoffmann et al. (2022) found that parameters and tokens should grow in equal proportion, at about 20 training tokens per parameter. Their 70-billion-parameter model Chinchilla, trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, outperformed the 280-billion-parameter Gopher, which was trained with about the same compute.
>
> Scaling laws predict the loss, the average next-token cross-entropy. They do not directly predict which tasks a model will be able to do (Section 5.6). In practice labs now often train smaller models on more data than the compute-optimal balance suggests, because a smaller model is cheaper to run once deployed.

**Formula.** The Chinchilla fit, where E is the loss that remains with unlimited parameters and data:

```latex
L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}}
```

**Image.** Log-log plot of loss against compute for a family of model sizes; each curve flattens, and the compute-optimal frontier is drawn along their lower edge.

**Interactive: "Spend a compute budget".**

- Display: predicted loss from the Chinchilla fit; the curve of loss against model size at the current budget, with the current choice and the optimum marked.
- Controls: compute budget (log slider, 10¹⁸ to 10²⁶ FLOPs); share of the budget spent on parameters versus tokens (sets N and D with C = 6ND); a switch that adds the cost of serving the model to many users, which moves the optimum toward smaller models.
- Try: (1) At Chinchilla's budget (about 5.8 × 10²³ FLOPs), compare N = 70 billion with N = 280 billion. (2) Multiply the budget by 10 and see how the optimal N changes. (3) Switch on serving cost.
- What to notice: the optimal model size grows roughly as the square root of compute.
- Build: live (formula with the published fitted constants); small.

**Check.** You get 100 times more compute. Roughly how should model size and data change? Answer: about 10 times each.

**Links.** Back to 1.5; forward to 5.6. References: Kaplan et al. (2020); Hoffmann et al. (2022).

**Changes made while building (2026-10-16).**

- The tool uses the unrounded Approach 3 constants (E = 1.6934, A = 406.4, B = 410.7, alpha = 0.3392, beta = 0.2849), from the paper's source as reported by Besiroglu et al. (2024). These reproduce the paper's own statements about the fit (a = 0.46 in Table 2; 40B parameters at Gopher's budget, Figure 4). The rounded values printed in the paper (1.69, 0.34, 0.28) give a = 0.452 and a 32B optimum at 5.76e23 FLOPs. The page shows the rounded values in the text and explains this in a sidenote.
- With C = 6ND the fit gives a = beta/(alpha + beta) = 0.4565, so the optimal N grows as C^0.46: 10x compute gives N x2.86 and D x3.50; 100x gives N x8.2 and D x12.2.
- The fit does not give about 20 tokens per parameter. At 5.76e23 FLOPs its optimum is 40.3B parameters and 2.38T tokens (59 per parameter), and the ratio grows with the budget (19 at 1e18, 93 at 1e26). The "about 20" comes from the paper's Approaches 1 and 2 (exponents 0.50 and 0.49), and is how Chinchilla was sized. Besiroglu et al. (2024) found the published Approach 3 fit poorly converged; their refit gives about 20. The page states all of this after the tasks.
- At 5.76e23 FLOPs: 70B gets 1.37T tokens and loss 1.922; 280B gets 0.34T tokens and loss 1.959.
- The Chinchilla-versus-Gopher result moved below the tasks, so task 1 is not answered in advance.
- The "share of budget" control is a model-size slider (D follows from C = 6ND), plus dragging on the chart and 70B, 280B, and best-size buttons.
- Serving cost: the budget must also pay 2N FLOPs per served token (slider from 1e10 to 1e14 tokens). At 5.76e23 with 1e13 tokens served, the best size falls from 40B to 12.5B. Sources: Touvron et al. (2023), Sardana et al. (2024).
- The check question now asks the reader to compute: 100x Chinchilla's compute at 20 tokens per parameter gives 700B parameters on 14T tokens. A second check asks what a lower predicted loss says about one task.
- The static figure is computed from the fit and labeled as such.
