## 3.1 Tokens

**Aim.** The reader knows that a language model reads and writes tokens (frequent chunks of text), how byte-pair encoding chooses them, and three consequences: trouble with spelling, trouble with arithmetic, and higher cost for languages that are rare in the training data.

**Text (draft).**

> A language model does not see letters or words. It sees tokens: chunks of text from a fixed vocabulary, often whole common words, pieces of rarer words, punctuation, and spaces. GPT-2's vocabulary has 50,257 tokens, and each token is identified by an integer.
>
> The vocabulary is built by byte-pair encoding. Start with single bytes. Find the most frequent adjacent pair in a large body of text and add it to the vocabulary as a new token. Repeat until the vocabulary reaches the target size.
>
> Tokenization explains some odd behavior. A model asked how many r's are in "strawberry" sees two or three tokens, not ten letters. Numbers are split into tokens unevenly, which makes arithmetic harder. Languages that are rare in the training data need more tokens per word, so text in them costs more to process and less of it fits in the model's context.

**Image.** The sentence "Unbelievably, the model tokenizes strawberry." split into colored tokens with their integer ids underneath.

**Interactive: "Tokenizer playground".**

- Display: the reader's text split into colored tokens with ids; token count; characters per token.
- Controls: a text box; tokenizer choice (GPT-2, and one current tokenizer); a second tab, "Build your own", with a tiny corpus and a Merge button that adds the most frequent pair to the vocabulary.
- Try: (1) Type 12345678 and see how it splits. (2) Type the same sentence in English and in Persian and compare token counts. (3) In the second tab, merge until "the" becomes a single token.
- What to notice: the model's units are statistical chunks, not words or letters.
- Build: live, using a JavaScript tokenizer library; small.

**Check.** Why might a model have trouble reversing the letters of a word? Answer: it sees the word as one or two tokens, not as a sequence of letters.

**Links.** Forward to 3.2, 3.3. References: Sennrich, Haddow, and Birch (2016).

**Changes made while building (2026-10-16).**

- The current tokenizer is GPT-4o's, o200k_base (199,998 ordinary tokens, so "about 200,000"). Both vocabularies run in the browser through js-tiktoken 1.0.21 (MIT), vendored in `docs/vendor/js-tiktoken-1.0.21/`. GPT-2's 50,257 = 256 bytes + 50,000 merges + 1 end-of-text token, checked in `tools/test-page-3-1.mjs`.
- "Strawberry" is one GPT-2 token with a leading space (" strawberry", id 41236) and three without (st, raw, berry). The draft's "two or three tokens" became "one id, or three", and a task now shows the difference.
- The three consequences (spelling, arithmetic, languages) moved below the tasks, so the tasks are not answered in advance. The arithmetic claim cites Singh and Strouse (2024); the language claim cites Petrov et al. (2023, up to 15 times).
- The Persian example is "من کتاب خواندن را دوست دارم." for "I like reading books." (GPT-2: 29 tokens against 5; GPT-4o: 9 against 5). Aydin should confirm the Persian.
- The two tabs are two stacked panels, as on page 2.5. Tokens are shown as outlined boxes with ids, not colored chips, to keep color for meaning. Bytes that are only part of a character are shown in italic hexadecimal.
- "Build your own" uses characters in place of bytes and splits words the way GPT-2 does (leading space kept). Ties are broken by first appearance. On the default corpus, " the" becomes one token after exactly 3 merges (t+h 13, th+e 12, space+the 10).
- Added a task (strawberry with and without a space) and a second check question (how much Persian fits in GPT-2's 1,024-token window). The first check now asks what the input says about the letters, instead of asking for recall.
