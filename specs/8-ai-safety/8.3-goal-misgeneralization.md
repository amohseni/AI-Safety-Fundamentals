## 8.3 Goal misgeneralization

**Aim.** The reader can tell goal misgeneralization (the reward was right, but the system learned a different goal, which shows up only in new situations) from reward hacking, and sees why it is hard to detect during training.

**Text (draft).**

> In reward hacking, the reward was wrong. In goal misgeneralization, the reward was right, and the system still learned the wrong goal.
>
> In the game CoinRun, an agent is rewarded for reaching a coin, which in every training level sits at the far right end of the level. Langosco et al. (2022) moved the coin to random positions at test time. The agent ignored the coin and ran to the right end. During training, "go to the coin" and "go to the right end" produced the same behavior and the same reward, so training had no reason to prefer one over the other, and the agent learned the one that was easier to learn.
>
> The general pattern: any training distribution is consistent with several goals, and a system may learn one that agrees with ours only on that distribution. The difference shows up only in new situations, possibly after deployment. Shah et al. (2022) give further examples. This is the behavioral side of the attribution problem in Section 7.1.

**Image.** Two CoinRun-style panels: a training level with the coin at the right end and the agent reaching it; a test level with the coin in the middle and the agent running past it.

**Interactive: "Coin or right end?"**

- Display: a simplified grid version of CoinRun; the agent's test success rate; a heat map of where the agent goes on test levels.
- Controls: the percentage of training levels in which the coin is not at the right end (0%, 2%, 10%, 50%); test level generator.
- Data: policies trained in advance for each setting.
- Try: (1) Train with 0% and test. (2) Find the smallest percentage at which the agent goes for the coin.
- What to notice: a little variety in training removes the ambiguity; without it, both goals fit the training data equally well.
- Build: precomputed policies; medium.

**Check.** During training, could you have told from the reward alone which goal the CoinRun agent had learned? Answer: no. Both goals earned the same reward on every training level.

**Links.** Back to 5.5, 7.1; forward to 8.5. References: Langosco et al. (2022), "Goal Misgeneralization in Deep Reinforcement Learning"; Shah et al. (2022), "Goal Misgeneralization: Why Correct Specifications Aren't Enough for Correct Goals".

**Changes made while building (2026-10-16).**

- The policies are trained live in the browser (6,000 REINFORCE episodes, about half a second, in slices with a progress readout), not precomputed. Ten fixed training seeds are available through "New training run". The agent sees the coin's direction and its own position, which lets both goals ("go to the coin", "go to the right end of the middle row") be represented; with coin-direction inputs alone, the agent cannot represent "go to the right end" except through a bias, which made the toy too contrived.
- Measured numbers (`tools/test-page-8-3.mjs`): with 0% randomized levels every run reaches the coin on every standard level, and test success is 22% to 30% across ten runs, with the agent reaching the right end in 75% to 81% of test levels. With 2%, nine of ten runs reach 84% to 99% and one reaches 32%. With 10%, every run reaches at least 95%. Training samples actions, so last-bit floating-point differences between JavaScript engines can change a run slightly; the page's ranges cover Node and Chromium.
- The draft's description of Langosco et al.'s result moved below the tasks, since it answers task 1. The page says what they found about randomizing 2% of training levels.
- The Shah et al. example given is the cultural transmission agent that followed an anti-expert partner, checked against the paper.
- A second check question asks the reader to design the test that would separate two goals in a new case.
