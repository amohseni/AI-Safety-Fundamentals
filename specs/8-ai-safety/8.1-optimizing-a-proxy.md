## 8.1 Optimizing a proxy (Goodhart's law)

**Aim.** The reader understands that strong optimization of a measure correlated with a goal tends to break the correlation; can distinguish two mechanisms (selecting on error, and pushing beyond the range where the correlation held); and sees that heavy-tailed errors make the problem much worse.

**Text (draft).**

> Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. In AI the measure is a reward function, a reward model, a test suite, or a benchmark, and the target is what we actually want.
>
> Manheim and Garrabrant (2018) distinguish several mechanisms, two of which matter most here. In the first, called regressional Goodhart, the proxy equals the true value plus an error. The options with the highest proxy scores are disproportionately those with large positive errors, so harder selection selects more error. In the second, called extremal Goodhart, the proxy and the goal are correlated over ordinary options but come apart at the extremes that a strong optimizer reaches.
>
> How bad regressional Goodhart is depends on the distribution of the error. If errors are light-tailed, such as normally distributed, harder selection still improves the true value, though by less than the proxy suggests. If errors are heavy-tailed, the most extreme proxy scores are mostly error, and harder optimization can yield no gain in true value at all (Kwa et al. 2024). The overoptimization curve in Section 4.3 is an instance of this problem.

**Image.** A scatter plot of proxy against true value for 10,000 options, with the top 1% by proxy highlighted: far to the right on the proxy axis, only modestly up on the true-value axis.

**Interactive: "Select on the proxy".** One of the first eight tools to build.

- Tab 1, regressional: N options with true value V drawn from a normal distribution and proxy U = V + error. Display: the scatter with the selected option marked; the expected true value and expected proxy value of the selected option, plotted against selection strength.
- Controls: error distribution (normal; Student's t with adjustable degrees of freedom; a mixture with rare huge errors); correlation; selection strength (best 1 of k, with k on a log slider from 1 to 10⁶).
- Tab 2, extremal: a proxy fitted on a limited range of inputs, then optimized beyond that range.
- Try: (1) With normal errors, increase selection strength: the true value keeps rising, but more slowly than the proxy. (2) Switch to heavy tails and find the selection strength at which the true value stops rising. (3) In tab 2, push the optimizer past the fitted range.
- What to notice: the gap between proxy and truth grows with optimization pressure, and the tails of the error decide whether optimizing helps at all.
- Build: live; medium.

**Check.** You pick the best of a million candidates using a reward model whose errors are heavy-tailed. What should you expect? Answer: the winner is likely there mainly because of a large error, and its true value may be ordinary.
