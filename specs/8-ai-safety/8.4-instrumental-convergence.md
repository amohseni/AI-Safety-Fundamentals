## 8.4 Instrumental convergence

**Aim.** The reader knows the instrumental convergence thesis and the published results that make it precise: the corrigibility problem, the cost of optimizing an incomplete proxy, and the power-seeking theorems for optimal and for retargetable decision-makers.

**Text (draft).**

> Some intermediate goals help with almost any final goal. Omohundro (2008) argued that sufficiently capable goal-directed systems will tend to have "basic AI drives", among them preserving themselves, keeping their goals unchanged, and acquiring resources, unless they are designed otherwise. Bostrom (2012, 2014) stated the instrumental convergence thesis: some instrumental goals are convergent, because attaining them would raise the chance of realizing a wide range of final goals in a wide range of situations.
>
> Later work turned the argument into results. Soares et al. (2015) studied corrigibility: the property of an agent that does not resist correction or shutdown. An agent maximizing almost any utility function has a reason to prevent its shutdown and to resist changes to its utility function, and they showed that natural proposals for making an agent indifferent to its shutdown button fail. Zhuang and Hadfield-Menell (2020) modeled a person who cares about many attributes of the world and an agent that optimizes a proxy covering only some of them. With limited resources, optimizing the incomplete proxy drives the omitted attributes to their worst values, so the person's true utility can end up arbitrarily low.
>
> Turner et al. (2021) proved a version of the thesis for Markov decision processes, a standard model of sequential decisions. For most reward functions drawn from a broad distribution, optimal policies tend to seek power: they move toward states from which more outcomes remain reachable. The result is strongest when the agent cares about the long run. Turner and Tadepalli (2022) extended it from optimal policies to a broad class of decision procedures whose targets can be reset, called retargetable, which includes some learning algorithms: for most settings of their targets, these procedures choose options that keep more outcomes available.
>
> All of these results describe idealized agents. Whether, and how strongly, trained systems show these tendencies is an empirical question (9.1).

- What to notice: without knowing an agent's goal, you can predict where most optimal agents will go; and leaving one thing out of a proxy is enough for optimization to sacrifice it.

**Check.** Does the power-seeking theorem say a trained neural network will seek power? Answer: no. Turner et al. (2021) concerns optimal policies for randomly drawn rewards; Turner and Tadepalli (2022) widens the class of decision procedures, but trained systems still need not fall within its assumptions, and their goals are not random.
