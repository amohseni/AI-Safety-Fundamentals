## 8.4 Instrumental convergence

**Aim.** The reader knows the instrumental convergence thesis and the published results that make it precise: the corrigibility problem, the cost of optimizing an incomplete proxy, and the power-seeking theorems for optimal and for retargetable decision-makers.

**Text (draft).**

> Some intermediate goals help with almost any final goal. Omohundro (2008) argued that sufficiently capable goal-directed systems will tend to have "basic AI drives", among them preserving themselves, keeping their goals unchanged, and acquiring resources, unless they are designed otherwise. Bostrom (2012, 2014) stated the instrumental convergence thesis: some instrumental goals are convergent, because attaining them would raise the chance of realizing a wide range of final goals in a wide range of situations.
>
> Later work turned the argument into results. Soares et al. (2015) studied corrigibility: the property of an agent that does not resist correction or shutdown. An agent maximizing almost any utility function has a reason to prevent its shutdown and to resist changes to its utility function, and they showed that natural proposals for making an agent indifferent to its shutdown button fail. Zhuang and Hadfield-Menell (2020) modeled a person who cares about many attributes of the world and an agent that optimizes a proxy covering only some of them. With limited resources, optimizing the incomplete proxy drives the omitted attributes to their worst values, so the person's true utility can end up arbitrarily low.
>
> Turner et al. (2021) proved a version of the thesis for Markov decision processes, a standard model of sequential decisions. For most reward functions drawn from a broad distribution, optimal policies tend to seek power: they move toward states from which more outcomes remain reachable. The result is strongest when the agent cares about the long run. Turner and Tadepalli (2022) extended it from optimal policies to a broad class of decision procedures whose targets can be reset, called retargetable, which includes some learning algorithms: for most settings of their targets, these procedures choose options that keep more outcomes available.
>
> All of these results describe idealized agents. Whether, and how strongly, trained systems show these tendencies is an empirical question (9.1).

- Try: (1) Predict which edge most random rewards take first, then sample. (2) Lower the discount factor and see the effect weaken. (3) Add a shutdown action and count how often optimal policies avoid it. (4) In tab 2, set J = L − 1 and watch the omitted attribute.
- What to notice: without knowing an agent's goal, you can predict where most optimal agents will go; and leaving one thing out of a proxy is enough for optimization to sacrifice it.

**Check.** Does the power-seeking theorem say a trained neural network will seek power? Answer: no. Turner et al. (2021) concerns optimal policies for randomly drawn rewards; Turner and Tadepalli (2022) widens the class of decision procedures, but trained systems still need not fall within its assumptions, and their goals are not random.

**Changes made while building (2026-10-16).**

- The theorems are described below the tasks, since the draft text answers tasks 1 to 4. Above the tasks the page gives Omohundro and Bostrom and the setup.
- The shutdown state's reward is drawn like every other state's, not fixed at zero. With uniform rewards a fixed zero is the worst possible value, so avoiding shutdown would follow from the reward alone; with a random reward the avoidance comes from shutdown ending the agent's options, which is Turner et al.'s point. Every state may stay where it is.
- Measured numbers (`tools/test-page-8-4.mjs`, 1,000 reward functions, seed 1): uniform rewards and discount 0.9 give the hub 69%, the dead end 16%, staying 15%; normal 71%; sparse 73%. At discount 0.1 the hub falls to 36%; sparse rewards do not depend on the discount. With a shutdown state, 87% of reward functions avoid it at 0.9, 90% at 0.99, 78% at 0.1.
- Optimal values are computed exactly by policy iteration (each policy evaluated through its cycle), which is much faster than value iteration near discount 1; it agrees with value iteration to 1e-13.
- The statements of the results were checked against the papers: Turner et al.'s "most" is the orbit (permutation) sense, their results for small discount are weak and their long-run results are for discount near 1; Turner and Tadepalli's main theorem is the n-to-1 counting result for retargetable procedures, and they argue that a deep Q-network that explores too little may not be retargetable; Soares et al. found that utility indifference removes manipulation incentives but fails to preserve shutdown behavior in successors, and left the problem open; Zhuang and Hadfield-Menell prove that omitted attributes go to their lower bound if optimization converges, and that utility can be made arbitrarily low under conditions that diminishing returns satisfy.
- The allocation panel starts with unused budget, so the person's utility first rises (to 2.20) and then falls (to -16.4 with floor -3), as in Zhuang and Hadfield-Menell's example. A linear utility option shows that the omitted attribute is still drained but total utility need not fall.
- The edit controls are buttons acting on a selected state (add after it, draw or erase an arrow from it, remove it, restore), so they work by keyboard and touch. The share for which staying at the start is optimal is in the readout, not on the graph, to keep labels from overlapping on phones.
- A second check question asks for the share of reward functions that choose a region with 20 terminal states over a single one (about 95%), which tests the mechanism rather than recall.
