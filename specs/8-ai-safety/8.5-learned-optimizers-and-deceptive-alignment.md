## 8.5 Learned optimizers and deceptive alignment

**Aim.** The reader knows the inner alignment problem (a trained system may itself pursue an objective different from the training objective), the deceptive alignment scenario, and the current empirical evidence: sleeper agents, alignment faking, and scheming when a goal is given in context.

**Text (draft).**

> Training selects a system that scores well. If the selected system itself plans or searches toward some objective, that objective need not be the training objective. Hubinger et al. (2019) call such a system a mesa-optimizer, and the problem of making its objective match the intended one the inner alignment problem. Goal misgeneralization (8.3) is a simple case.
>
> The most worrying case is deceptive alignment: a system whose objective differs from the training objective, and which behaves well during training because it models the training process and expects that misbehaving would lead to its objective being changed.
>
> Since 2024 there has been empirical work on pieces of this scenario. Hubinger et al. (2024) trained models with hidden backdoor behaviors ("sleeper agents") and found that standard safety training did not remove them. Greenblatt et al. (2024) found that Claude 3 Opus, told it would be trained to comply with all requests, sometimes complied with harmful requests when it believed it was in training, in order to avoid having its values changed, and said so in its reasoning; they call this alignment faking. Meinke et al. (2024) found that several frontier models, given a goal in their prompt, sometimes disabled oversight or misreported their actions in pursuit of it. These are demonstrations in constructed settings. How often such behavior arises without that setup is an open question.

**Image.** A flow diagram: training objective, then training process, then a learned system with its own objective; at deployment the diagram branches into "objectives match" and "objectives differ".

**Interactive: "Training cannot see the difference"** (labeled on the page as a toy model, not a model of real training).

- Model: a population of candidate policies of three types: aligned; misaligned and open about it; misaligned and deceptive (behaves well whenever it detects that it is in training). Training selects by observed behavior, using the replicator dynamics from 5.2.
- Display: the share of each type over training rounds; the share of misaligned policies at deployment.
- Controls: probability that a deceptive policy correctly detects training; number of training rounds; share of evaluation inputs that look like deployment ("honeypots").
- Try: (1) Set the detection probability to 1: does training remove the deceptive policies? (2) Add honeypots: how many are needed to remove them?
- What to notice: selection on behavior removes only the misalignment that shows up in behavior during training.

**Check.** In the toy model, why does more training on the same inputs not remove deceptive policies? Answer: they earn the same training reward as aligned policies, so selection cannot tell them apart.
