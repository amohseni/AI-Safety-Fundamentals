## 8.6 Oversight and control

**Aim.** The reader knows the main research approaches for keeping AI systems safe when they may be more capable than their overseers (scalable oversight, evaluations, AI control and monitoring, and interpretability-based audits) and what each one assumes.

**Text (draft).**

> Training with human feedback works when humans can judge the outputs. As systems become more capable, that assumption weakens. The research approaches differ in what they assume.
>
> Scalable oversight tries to let weaker overseers supervise stronger systems. In debate (Irving, Christiano, and Amodei 2018), two AI systems argue opposite sides of a question and a human judges; the hope is that exposing a false claim is easier than checking a true one unaided. Weak-to-strong generalization (Burns et al. 2023) studies a small model supervising a large one, as an analogy for humans supervising more capable models.
>
> Evaluations measure what models can do, including dangerous capabilities, before deployment. METR measures the length of tasks, in human working time, that models can complete; that length doubled roughly every seven months from 2019 to 2025 (Kwa et al. 2025).
>
> AI control (Greenblatt et al. 2024) assumes the model might be misaligned and designs deployment procedures that remain safe anyway, such as monitoring by a weaker trusted model and limits on the model's permissions. Monitoring the chain of thought is one such tool, useful only while the reasoning stays readable and faithful (4.4). Interpretability-based audits (Section 6) try to find hidden objectives directly; Marks et al. (2025) ran an exercise in which teams searched a model for an objective that had been deliberately trained into it.

**Image.** A 2 by 2 grid. Rows: "assumes the model is aligned" and "allows that it may not be". Columns: "humans can judge the outputs" and "humans cannot". Approaches placed in cells: RLHF; debate and weak-to-strong; evaluations; AI control and audits.

**Interactive: "Control protocol game".**

- Model: an untrusted model completes 1,000 tasks and secretly attempts sabotage on a few. A trusted weaker monitor gives each task a suspicion score. Humans can audit a fixed share of tasks.
- Controls: monitor quality (how well its scores separate sabotage from normal work); audit budget (for example, 2% of tasks); audit threshold; a second threshold above which the task is handed to the trusted model instead.
- Display: safety (probability that no sabotage succeeds) against usefulness (share of tasks done by the strong model), with the current protocol marked.
- Try: (1) With a 2% audit budget, find the threshold that maximizes safety. (2) Add hand-offs to the trusted model and trace the trade-off between safety and usefulness. (3) Make the monitor worse and see what the protocol can still guarantee.
- What to notice: safety can be bought without trusting the model, at a cost in usefulness.
- Build: live simulation; medium.

**Check.** What does AI control assume that alignment through human feedback does not? Answer: that the model may be actively trying to get around oversight.

**Changes made while building (2026-10-16).**

- The protocol is defined on the page and computed exactly by dynamic programming in `docs/js/lib/s7-control.js`, checked against Monte Carlo (40,000 runs per setting) in `tools/test-page-8-6.mjs`: 1,000 tasks, sabotage on exactly 5 at random positions; scores Normal(0, 1) for normal work and Normal(μ, 1) for sabotage, μ = monitor quality; audit if the score is at least the audit threshold while the budget lasts (a caught attempt ends the run safely); otherwise hand off to the trusted model if the score is at least the hand-off threshold; otherwise accept. Safety is the probability that no sabotage succeeds; usefulness is the expected share of tasks done by the untrusted model in an honest run (audited tasks count as its work). This is simpler than the paper's usefulness, which is rescaled correctness; the page says so.
- Task 1's answer is not the threshold that flags 2%: with a 2% budget and μ = 2, safety peaks at threshold 1.64 (flags 5.1%, safety 57.9%) against 48.0% at 2.05, because the run is usually decided by the first attempt (on average near task 167) while the budget lasts about 400 tasks. The notice states that this depends on the model attacking at random times.
- Measured: hand-off at 1.0 (audit 2.05): safety 75.1%, usefulness 86.0%; safety never falls and usefulness never rises as the hand-off threshold falls. With μ = 0.5, 99% safety first needs hand-off threshold −2.34 (usefulness 2.8%), and without hand-offs the best is 15.0%; with μ = 2, 99% safety costs usefulness down to 29.9%.
