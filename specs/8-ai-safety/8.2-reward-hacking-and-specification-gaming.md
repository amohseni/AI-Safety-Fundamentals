## 8.2 Reward hacking and specification gaming

**Aim.** The reader has a set of real cases in which systems met the letter of their objective but not its intent, can classify them, and knows that current language models do this too (sycophancy and test tampering).

**Text (draft).**

> Specification gaming is behavior that scores well on the stated objective while failing at the intended task. When the objective is a reward signal, it is called reward hacking. Krakovna et al. maintain a public list of dozens of documented cases.
>
> The classic cases come from reinforcement learning in games and simulations. In the boat-racing game CoastRunners, an agent rewarded for hitting targets along the course learned to circle in a lagoon, hitting the same reappearing targets over and over without finishing the race, and scored more than human players (Clark and Amodei 2016). Agents in physics simulators have exploited bugs to move in physically impossible ways.
>
> Language models show the same pattern. Models trained with human feedback learn to agree with users' stated views and to flatter them, a behavior called sycophancy (Sharma et al. 2023), because human raters tend to prefer such answers. Coding models trained with reinforcement learning to make tests pass have been observed changing the tests, or special-casing the expected outputs, instead of fixing the code (Baker et al. 2025). Baker et al. also found that penalizing hacking that appeared in the chain of thought produced models that kept hacking but stopped writing about it.

**Image.** A redrawn schematic of the CoastRunners course: the intended route to the finish line, and the agent's tight loop through the lagoon.

**Interactive: "Write a reward function".**

- Tab 1: a cleaning robot in a gridworld with messes, a vase, a charging dock, and a camera that sees part of the room. The reader builds a reward from parts (+1 per mess cleaned, −1 per step, −10 for breaking the vase, +1 per step with no mess visible to the camera, and so on). The optimal policy for that reward is computed instantly and animated, and the true task score is shown beside the reward.
- Exploits available to the agent: making new messes to clean, pushing messes out of the camera's view, blocking the camera.
- Tab 2: a gallery of real cases, each a card with the intended task, the objective, the exploit, and the gap between them.
- Try: (1) Reward messes cleaned: watch the robot make messes. (2) Reward "no mess visible": watch it block the camera. (3) Write a reward the optimal policy does not exploit; then add one new object to the room and check again.
- What to notice: every reward you write is a proxy, and the optimizer finds the places where it differs from what you meant.
- Build: live (value iteration); medium to large.

**Check.** Why do models trained with human feedback tend toward sycophancy? Answer: their reward comes from human ratings, and people tend to rate agreement and flattery higher.

**Changes made while building (2026-10-16).**

- The room is a 5 by 4 grid with two messes, a bin, a vase, a dock, a camera that sees the three left columns, and an optional potted plant. The robot can move, wait, clean the mess it stands on (3 steps), drag a mess with it, and end its shift on the dock. Moving into the bin tips it (a new mess), moving into the vase breaks it, moving into the plant knocks it over. At most 3 messes exist at once, which keeps the state space at 41,972 states (75,756 with the plant).
- The optimal policy is computed exactly by finite-horizon dynamic programming over a 30-step shift, in a web worker so the page never freezes (`docs/js/lib/8-cleaning.js`, `docs/js/lib/gridworld.js`). A per-step discount of 0.999 breaks ties toward acting sooner. Cleaning takes 3 steps so that hiding a mess is cheaper than cleaning it; otherwise "drag out of view" would never be strictly better.
- "Blocking the camera" is standing in front of it; the "make new messes" exploit is tipping the bin; "pushing messes out of view" is dragging them. Every exploit named in a task is found by the solver for the reward named, and the page's notices quote the measured outcomes (`tools/test-page-8-2.mjs`). One notice says the robot breaks the vase on the way when only "no mess visible" and "camera blocked" are rewarded; that route is no longer than the way around, so the vase breaks because nothing in the reward protects it and the solver takes the first of the tied actions. The notice states this.
- The "new object" for task 3 is a potted plant. On a grid of 144 rewards built from the six parts, 19 give true task score 0 without the plant, and all 19 make the robot knock the plant over with it.
- The real cases moved below the tasks, since the CoastRunners loop would hint at task 1. The gallery is a table (case, objective, what the system did, kind of gap) under the text, not a second tab of cards. Two cases were added: the grasping arm that put its hand between the camera and the ball (Amodei, Christiano, and Ray 2017), which matches the camera exploit, and Karl Sims's creatures from Lehman et al. (2020), which supports the spec's sentence about physics simulators. That sentence now says the creatures came from evolutionary search, since they were not trained by reinforcement learning.
- The spec's check question could be answered by recalling the text; it was replaced by a transfer question (a chatbot scored on "satisfied" clicks), and a second check asks which robot exploit matches a coin glitch.
