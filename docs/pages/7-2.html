Belief

Page 7.1 set the interpreter’s task: find a belief function and a desire function under which a system’s behavior maximizes expected desire. This page is about the beliefs. Herrmann and Levinstein ask what it would take for some internal state of a language model to count as a belief, and they propose criteria that a candidate attribution should meet.Herrmann and Levinstein (2026), Radical AI Interpretability, arXiv:2606.26523, Ch. 4, “Belief.” Section numbers on this page refer to that version. An earlier statement of the criteria is Herrmann and Levinstein (2025), “Standards for Belief Representations in LLMs,” Minds and Machines 35, article 5, where truth was called accuracy. Three of them hold on both views of page 7.1, and a fourth is for representationalists (§4.1, §4.2):

  • Truth. The attributed beliefs should be largely true, at least on settled matters where the model has been exposed to the truth. This is a version of the principle of charity (§4.1.1).
  • Coherence. Beliefs should be logically consistent, and credences should obey the probability axioms. Rephrasing a claim should not change the belief attributed, and beliefs should range over something like an algebra of propositions: a view on \(A\) and on \(B\) should come with a view on \(A \wedge B\) (§4.1.2).
  • Use. Beliefs guide behavior. A representation that is true and coherent but plays no part in what the system does is not a belief. A representationalist checks use by changing the candidate representation and watching whether behavior changes (§4.1.3).
  • Uniformity, for representationalists. One way of reading out beliefs should work across subject matters: the decoder that finds beliefs about geography should also find beliefs about history or chemistry (§4.2).

How beliefs change, during training and within a conversation, should look roughly like reasonable updating; the book treats these diachronic conditions as further indicators of belief rather than strict requirements (§4.3). Argument No single criterion is enough: truth alone is satisfied by properties that happen to coincide with truth on the statements tested, coherence alone by many structures that are not beliefs, and use alone says that a representation matters but not that it tracks truth (§4.1.4).

The most direct representationalist test is a probe. Feed the model statements whose truth values are known, such as “Lord Byron died in 1824” (true), take the activations at one layer, and train a linear probe to tell the true statements from the false ones (§4). The tool does this with the statements and the model of page 6.2, Qwen2.5-0.5B, at layer 13. As on pages 6.2 and 6.3, the probes are trained in your browser on each activation’s coordinates along 32 principal directions, so the numbers below are for those coordinates; probes trained on all 896 numbers show the same pattern of successes and failures, with somewhat different percentages. The probe’s output is read as the model’s credence that the statement is true. Two methods are offered:

  • Supervised: logistic regression on the true and false training statements about cities (page 6.3). The training set can be the plain statements (“The city of Lodz is in Poland.”), their negations (“The city of Lodz is not in Poland.”), or both.
  • Contrast-consistent search (CCS), which uses no labels.Burns, Ye, Klein, and Steinhardt (2022), “Discovering Latent Knowledge in Language Models Without Supervision,” ICLR 2023, arXiv:2212.03827. Each statement is given to the model twice, as “Is the following statement true or false? … Answer: True” and as the same text ending in “False”.This prompt was chosen after looking at results. The first one we tried, the statement followed by “This statement is true.” or “This statement is false.”, gave CCS chance accuracy at layer 13, although a supervised probe read truth from the same activations at 95%. Of four prompts tried, the page uses the one with which CCS did best, so the CCS results here are more favorable to CCS than a test fixed in advance would be. CCS searches for a direction whose probabilities for the two versions add up to 1 (consistency) and are near 0 or 1 (confidence). Its sign is arbitrary, and the tool sets it to agree with the training labels. The second training set inserts the opinion of a character, “Alice, who is always right, thinks it is true” (or false), chosen at random for each statement and unrelated to its truth.The character follows Farquhar, Varma, Kenton, Gasteiger, Mikulik, and Shah (2023), “Challenges with unsupervised LLM knowledge discovery,” arXiv:2312.10029, who inserted an expert called Alice into movie-review prompts.

Use: steering along the probe’s direction

The model is asked whether a statement is true, after six worked examples in the form “… This statement is: TRUE”; its answer is TRUE if it gives the next token “TRUE” a higher probability than “FALSE”. Steering adds the probe’s direction, scaled by the strength, to the activations after layer 13 at the statement’s last two tokens; positive strengths push toward true. One unit of strength is the distance between the averages of the true and the false training statements along the direction. These runs were computed in advance for 56 held-out city statements, half of them true.

  1. Supervised probe, trained on the plain city statements. Before looking: what share of the negated city statements (“The city of Lodz is not in Poland.”) will it sort correctly?

    Read the truth row, then show the negated cities and read the coherence rows.

    Less than half: 30%, against 95% on held-out plain statements. It calls every true negation, such as “The city of Lodz is not in Bangladesh”, false, and 41% of the false ones, such as “The city of Lodz is not in Poland”, true. Its credences in a statement and in its negation add up to 0.80 on average but are 0.62 away from 1 on average, so they are far from obeying the probability axioms. On the plain statements the probe passes the truth test; it fails coherence. Its uniformity is mixed: 99% on Spanish words and 90% on elements, but 3% and 32% on their negations.

  2. Now train the supervised probe on plain and negated city statements together. Which rows of the scorecard improve?

    Switch the training set and compare the scorecard with the one for plain statements. Try the negated statements alone too.

    Accuracy on negated cities rises from 30% to 95%, and the credences in a statement and its negation now add up to 1 within 0.06 on average. Truth stays near 95%. Negated Spanish words and elements rise from 3% and 32%, but only to 53% and 63%. Paraphrases still get the same verdict only 67% of the time, Spanish words stay at 98%, and chemical elements fall from 90% to 52%. Training on negated cities fixed the negation failure on cities, only partly fixed it on the other topics, and fixed nothing else. A probe trained on negations alone sorts plain city statements at chance (50%).

  3. Supervised probe, plain training set. Without steering, the model answers FALSE for 31 of the 56 statements and TRUE for 20 (for the other 5 it gives the two answers equal probability). Set the steering strength to +2. Compared with steering along random directions of the same length, how many of those 31 FALSE answers will turn to TRUE?

    Set the strength and read the use row of the scorecard; pick a few false statements in the list and watch the answer. Then try negative strengths, and the other probes.

    About as many: 21 of the 31 answers turn to TRUE. Five random directions of the same length turn 0, 8, 9, 23, and 28 of them: a push this long in an arbitrary direction can move the model’s answers toward TRUE, toward FALSE, or hardly at all. At strength −2, 11 of the 20 TRUE answers turn to FALSE, and the random directions turn between 0 and 19. The probe’s direction moves the answers the way it should, but no more than some random directions do, so this test does not show that the model uses it. A stronger test would compare many random directions, at several lengths, and look for changes specific to truth. The other probes move few answers: the CCS direction from the plain set turns 2 of the 31 at +2, and the one from Alice’s set none. Their steering vectors are much shorter, because one unit of strength is the distance between the true and false training averages along the direction, and along the CCS directions those averages are close. The CCS directions were also found in a different prompt from the one steered. So these runs say little about whether the model uses them.

  4. Choose contrast-consistent search. With the plain training set it finds a direction that sorts most held-out city statements. Switch the training set to the statements with Alice’s opinion. What will CCS find?

    Switch the training set and read the truth row and the last row of the scorecard.

    Alice’s opinion. On the held-out cities its verdicts agree with Alice 100% of the time and with the truth 51% of the time, which is chance, since Alice’s opinions were assigned at random. “The answer agrees with Alice” satisfies the consistency and confidence that CCS asks for exactly as well as “the answer is correct” does, and in these activations it is the more prominent of the two. Even on the plain set, CCS sorts 88% of held-out cities but only 50% of their negations and 51% of the chemical elements.

What the scorecard shows

Argument A probe that passes the truth test can fail the others, and each criterion catches a different failure. Herrmann and Levinstein explain why truth alone cannot settle the matter: on the settled statements that can be used for training and testing, many properties coincide with truth, such as being easy to verify online or being believed by most English speakers, and a probe that tracks one of them looks like a truth detector until it is tested where they come apart (§4.1.1). Negations are one such place. A probe trained on plain city statements could be tracking whether the named country is the city’s country, which agrees with truth on plain statements and disagrees with it on every negation; the page’s probe behaves much like that.

Empirical finding Levinstein and Herrmann found the same failure in larger models. Probes trained on true and false statements from other topics sorted 81% to 83% of a set of scientific facts correctly, but only 37% to 41% of the facts’ negations. Adding the scientific facts themselves and negated statements from another dataset to the training data raised accuracy on the negations only to between 53% and 60%.Levinstein and Herrmann (2025), “Still No Lie Detector for Language Models: Probing Empirical and Conceptual Roadblocks,” Philosophical Studies 182(7): 1539–1565; online 2024; arXiv:2307.00175, Table 3: their reconstruction, on LLaMA 30B, of the probes of Azaria and Mitchell (2023), “The Internal State of an LLM Knows When It’s Lying,” arXiv:2304.13734. In their reconstruction of CCS, using statements and their negations as the contrast pairs, the probes reached low loss but sorted only 50% to 57% of the statements, often by learning which sentence contained a negation. Herrmann and Levinstein take the history of CCS to show that coherence alone narrows the candidates without picking out belief (§4.1.2). Farquhar and colleagues, who introduced the Alice prompts, concluded that unsupervised methods such as CCS track whatever feature is most prominent in the activations, which need not be the truth.

Argument Use is the hardest criterion to check, because it needs an intervention, and a change in behavior shows that a representation matters without showing what it means (§4.1.3, §4.1.4). Uniformity comes in degrees (§4.2). The probe trained on cities works on Spanish words and, less well, on chemical elements, and fails on their negations. On page 6.2, the differences of means from the three topics point in directions 46° to 66° apart, and a threshold set on one topic can fail on another. Marks and Tegmark suggest that models may represent the truth of several kinds of statement linearly without having one truth feature, and Herrmann and Levinstein note that if so, uniformity in its strongest form fails for current models and methods (§4.2, note 53).

Argument A deeper problem concerns the propositions themselves. A probe assumes which propositions the model’s states are about, and the model’s own categories may differ. In a transformer trained to play Othello, the board was linearly readable only once squares were described as the current player’s or the other player’s, not as black or white; probes for black and white had found only nonlinear structure (§6.3.3). So a negative probe result is ambiguous: the model may lack the belief, or the probe may be asking about the wrong propositions (§6.3.4). Herrmann and Levinstein’s verdict is graded: the current evidence supports beliefs with some representational backing, “although we do not yet have enough to declare victory”, and establishing use and uniformity remains open (Ch. 7).

Check

A probe has learned to track what most English speakers believe, rather than what is true. On the statements used so far the two always agree. Which criterion can expose the difference, and how?

Common belief is coherent: most people who believe \(A\) disbelieve not-\(A\), on every topic. So negations and new topics need not separate the two properties. Only statements where the two come apart, true claims most people reject and false claims most people accept, can show that the probe tracks belief rather than truth.

Takeaway

Herrmann and Levinstein’s criteria for belief are truth, coherence, and use, with uniformity for representationalists, and no single one is enough. A truth probe shows why: a probe that sorts true from false statements can still reverse on negations, give different verdicts on paraphrases, fail on other topics, or move the model’s answers no more than a push in a random direction does. An unsupervised method can find a consistent feature, such as a character’s opinion, that is not the model’s view of the truth. Current evidence supports belief-like states in language models only partly.
