Learning as selection

An agent chooses among three actions with probabilities \(x_1, x_2, x_3\). Each choice earns a payoff between 0 and 1. In Cross’s rule, after choosing action \(i\) and receiving payoff \(u\), the agent moves its probabilities a fraction \(\alpha u\) of the way toward always choosing \(i\): \(x \leftarrow x + \alpha u\,(e_i - x)\), where \(e_i\) puts probability 1 on action \(i\) and \(\alpha\) is the step size.Cross (1973), “A stochastic learning model of economic behavior,” Quarterly Journal of Economics 87: 239–266. An action that pays well gets chosen, then gets more probability, then gets chosen more.

Evolutionary biology describes the same pattern in a population: types that do better than average become more common. The replicator equation says that the share \(x_i\) of type \(i\) grows at a rate proportional to how far its payoff \(\pi_i\) exceeds the average payoff \(\bar\pi\):

$$\dot{x}_i = x_i\,(\pi_i - \bar{\pi}), \qquad \bar{\pi} = \sum_j x_j \pi_j.$$

The tool compares learning runs with the solution of this equation. Besides Cross’s rule it has two other rules. Exponential weights keeps a score for each action, adds to every score the payoff that action would have earned in the round, and chooses with probabilities proportional to \(e^{\text{score}}\). Softmax policy gradient also chooses with probabilities proportional to \(e^{\theta_i}\), but after choosing \(i\) and receiving \(u\) it changes the parameters by \(\alpha u\,(e_i - x)\), the step of the REINFORCE algorithm. For these two rules, \(\alpha\) is the step size that multiplies each change of the scores or parameters.

  4. Switch to rock-paper-scissors, where the learner plays against opponents who choose as it does (win 1, tie 0.5, loss 0). What does the replicator curve do?

    Look at the ink curve, then run Cross’s rule at step sizes 0.01 and 0.001.

    It circles the center on a closed loop: along the curve, the product \(x_1 x_2 x_3\) stays at its starting value, 0.030. The learning runs drift outward instead. With step size 0.01, after 120 units of time the product is below 0.003 in all 10 runs, so the paths are near the edges; with step size 0.001 it is between 0.006 and 0.037. The replicator equation describes the learner over a bounded stretch of time, and the noise of learning can add up over a long one.

Why learning follows selection

Theorem In one step of Cross’s rule, the expected change of \(x_i\) is exactly \(\alpha\, x_i(\pi_i - \bar\pi)\): the chosen action gains in proportion to its payoff, every action loses in proportion to its probability, and the two effects net to the replicator equation. Börgers and Sarin proved more: with payoffs strictly between 0 and 1, as the step size shrinks while the number of steps per unit of time grows, the path of the learning process over any bounded stretch of time comes as close as desired to the solution of the replicator equation, with probability approaching 1.Börgers and Sarin (1997), “Learning through reinforcement and replicator dynamics,” Journal of Economic Theory 77: 1–14. Over unbounded time the learning process behaves differently: they show that it settles with probability 1 on always choosing a single action, which the replicator equation need not do. Task 4 shows the drift toward the edges that leads there.

Theorem Exponential weights gives exactly the replicator equation in continuous time. If each score grows at the rate \(\pi_i\) and \(x_i = e^{s_i}/\sum_j e^{s_j}\), then \(\dot x_i = x_i(\dot s_i - \sum_j x_j \dot s_j) = x_i(\pi_i - \bar\pi)\). Softmax policy gradient gives a related flow that is not the same. Its expected parameter change is \(\dot\theta_i = x_i(\pi_i - \bar\pi)\), the replicator term itself, and the probabilities then move by \(\dot x_i = x_i(\dot\theta_i - \sum_j x_j \dot\theta_j)\). So policy gradient is the replicator equation with each action’s advantage \(\pi_i - \bar\pi\) multiplied once more by its own probability \(x_i\). A rare action with a high payoff is therefore selected more slowly than under the replicator equation, which is why action 2 got ahead in task 3. The two flows have the same rest points.

Argument These results concern simple learning rules, not the training of language models. Their value here is as a way of seeing: learning from reward is a selection process. Behaviors that earn more reward than average become more common, whether or not anyone intended them. Sections 8.2 and 8.4 apply this way of seeing to reward hacking and instrumental convergence.Hofbauer and Sigmund (1998), Evolutionary Games and Population Dynamics, Cambridge University Press, treats the replicator equation in full.

Under the replicator equation, an action’s payoff equals the current average payoff. What happens to its probability at that moment?

Its rate of change is \(x_i(\pi_i - \bar\pi) = 0\). It may grow or shrink later, as the other probabilities change the average.

A learner trained by reward finds a behavior that exploits a flaw in how reward is computed, and the behavior earns more reward than average. Nobody designed it. What does the selection view predict?

Takeaway

With small steps, Cross’s reinforcement rule follows the replicator equation over bounded stretches of time, exponential weights gives exactly the replicator equation in continuous time, and softmax policy gradient gives a related flow that selects rare actions more slowly. Learning from reward is selection: whatever earns more than average spreads, whether or not anyone designed it.
