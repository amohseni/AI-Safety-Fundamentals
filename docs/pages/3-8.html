Scaling laws

Kaplan et al. (2020) trained many language models of different sizes and measured their loss on held-out text, the average next-token cross-entropy (Section 2.5).Kaplan et al. (2020), “Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models,” arXiv:2001.08361. Empirical finding The loss fell as a power law in the number of parameters \(N\), in the number of training tokens \(D\), and in the training compute \(C\), each as long as the other two were not the bottleneck, with some trends spanning more than seven orders of magnitude. On a plot with logarithmic axes, each of these relations is close to a straight line.

Training compute is about \(C \approx 6ND\) floating-point operations (FLOPs): about 6 for each parameter and each training token, counting the forward and backward passes. So a fixed budget can buy a large model trained on few tokens, or a small model trained on many. Hoffmann et al. (2022) trained over 400 models to find the best split, and fitted this formula for the loss:Hoffmann et al. (2022), “Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models,” NeurIPS; the fit is their Approach 3. The paper’s text rounds the constants. The tool uses the unrounded values from the paper’s source, \(E = 1.6934\), \(\alpha = 0.3392\), \(\beta = 0.2849\), as reported by Besiroglu et al. (2024), “Chinchilla Scaling: A replication attempt,” arXiv:2404.10102. They reproduce the paper’s own statements about this fit; the rounded values do not.

$$L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}},$$

with \(E \approx 1.69\), \(A = 406.4\), \(B = 410.7\), \(\alpha \approx 0.34\), and \(\beta \approx 0.28\). \(E\) is the loss that would remain with unlimited parameters and data. The second term shrinks as the model grows, and the third as the data grows. With \(C\) fixed, a larger \(N\) means a smaller \(D = C/6N\), so the two terms pull in opposite directions, and some model size gives the lowest loss.

    The best \(N\) grows about 2.9 times, from 40 to 115 billion, and the best \(D\) about 3.5 times. Both grow, and by similar factors. A tenfold budget split evenly between the two would multiply each by \(\sqrt{10} \approx 3.2\).

    Smaller: about 12.5 billion parameters instead of 40, trained on more tokens (4.4 trillion instead of 2.4). Every token served costs about \(2N\), so a smaller model leaves more of the budget for training. The more tokens served, the smaller the best model.

What Chinchilla showed

Empirical finding Hoffmann et al. tested their prediction with Chinchilla, a 70-billion-parameter model trained on 1.4 trillion tokens. It outperformed the 280-billion-parameter Gopher, trained on 300 billion tokens with about the same compute, on a large range of downstream tasks. Their three ways of estimating the best split all found that the best \(N\) and \(D\) both grow roughly as the square root of compute (exponents 0.50, 0.49, and 0.46 for \(N\)). The first two give about 20 tokens per parameter across the budgets they consider, which is how Chinchilla was sized: 1.4 trillion tokens for 70 billion parameters.

The fitted formula in the tool, their third way, gives a different number. At Chinchilla’s budget its best split is about 60 tokens per parameter, and the best ratio grows with the budget, from about 19 at \(10^{18}\) FLOPs to about 90 at \(10^{26}\). Empirical finding Besiroglu et al. (2024) refitted the formula to data they reconstructed from one of the paper’s figures, and found that the published fit had stopped before it converged; their refit agrees with the first two methods, at about 20 tokens per parameter. So the tool’s exact numbers come from one fitted formula, and the paper’s main result is the rule of about 20 tokens per parameter.

Predicted loss against training compute for five model sizes, from the fitted formula. Each curve flattens, because a model of fixed size cannot get below \(E + A/N^{\alpha}\) however long it trains. The compute-optimal frontier runs along their lower edge: for each budget, the size that reaches the lowest loss.

Argument Labs often train smaller models on more data than the compute-optimal split, because a smaller model is cheaper to run for every user once it is deployed.Touvron et al. (2023), “LLaMA: Open and Efficient Foundation Language Models,” arXiv:2302.13971, trained a 7-billion-parameter model on 1 trillion tokens for this reason. Sardana et al. (2024), “Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal: Accounting for Inference in Language Model Scaling Laws,” ICML, work out the split with serving costs included.

Scaling laws predict the loss, the average over all next tokens. They do not directly predict which tasks a model will be able to do; Section 5.6 looks at abilities that seem to appear suddenly as models grow.

Check

A lab has 100 times Chinchilla’s compute (70 billion parameters, 1.4 trillion tokens) and follows the rule of about 20 tokens per parameter, with \(C \approx 6ND\). Which model should it train?

With \(D = 20N\), compute is \(C \approx 120N^2\), so 100 times the compute means \(\sqrt{100} = 10\) times the parameters and 10 times the tokens.

A scaling law predicts that a new model ten times larger will have a lower loss. What does that tell you about whether it will be able to do three-digit multiplication?

The law predicts the next-token loss averaged over all the test text, and one task is a small part of that average. A lower average is compatible with large gains on some tasks and none on others.

Takeaway

A language model’s loss falls predictably, roughly as a power law, as parameters, data, and compute grow. With training compute about \(6ND\), a fixed budget has a best split between model size and data; Hoffmann et al. found that both should grow roughly as the square root of compute, at about 20 tokens per parameter. Counting the cost of serving moves the best choice toward smaller models trained on more data. Scaling laws predict the average loss, and do not directly predict specific abilities.
