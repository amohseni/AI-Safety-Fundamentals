Assistants and reasoning models

A model trained only to predict internet text continues documents. Given a question, it may answer it, or it may write more questions, because both happen in its training data. InstructGPT (2022) fine-tuned GPT-3 on example answers written by human labelers, and then trained it further with reinforcement learning, using a reward model fitted to the labelers’ rankings of its own answers. The method is called reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF (Sections 4.2 and 4.3). Empirical finding Labelers preferred the answers of a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT model to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3.Ouyang, Wu, Jiang, Almeida, Wainwright, Mishkin, Zhang, Agarwal, and others (2022), “Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback,” NeurIPS; arXiv:2203.02155. ChatGPT, released in November 2022, was trained with the same method and put in a chat interface.OpenAI (2022), “Introducing ChatGPT,” November 30, 2022. The post says the model was trained with RLHF “using the same methods as InstructGPT, but with slight differences in the data collection setup.”

In the same year, Wei et al. showed that prompting a model with worked examples that write out intermediate steps, called chain-of-thought prompting, improved its accuracy on arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning problems.Wei, Wang, Schuurmans, Bosma, Ichter, Xia, Chi, Le, and Zhou (2022), “Chain-of-thought prompting elicits reasoning in large language models,” NeurIPS; arXiv:2201.11903. Each written token is one more pass through the network, so writing out steps gives the model more computation per answer (Section 4.4).

Starting with OpenAI’s o1 (September 2024) and DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025), labs trained models with reinforcement learning on problems whose answers can be checked automatically, such as math and coding problems.OpenAI (2024), “Learning to reason with LLMs,” September 12, 2024. DeepSeek-AI (2025), “DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing reasoning capability in LLMs via reinforcement learning,” arXiv:2501.12948. These reasoning models learned to write long chains of thought before answering, to check their work, and to backtrack. In 2025, attention moved to agents: models that carry out tasks over many steps using tools.

The tool below shows real outputs from one model family, Qwen3, released by Alibaba in 2025.Yang, Li, Yang, Zhang, Hui, Zheng, Yu, Gao, and others (2025), “Qwen3 technical report,” arXiv:2505.09388. The outputs were generated once on a CPU with fixed random seeds; the models, revisions, and sampling settings are stored with them in data/1-6/outputs.json, and the script is tools/1-6/generate.py. The base model, Qwen3-0.6B-Base, was pretrained on about 36 trillion tokens and nothing else. The post-trained model, Qwen3-0.6B, can run in two modes: with thinking off it answers at once, as a chat model does; with thinking on it first writes a chain of thought, marked off from its answer, as a reasoning model does. Both modes use the same parameters. Qwen trained only its two largest models, Qwen3-235B-A22B and Qwen3-32B, with reinforcement learning, and then trained this small model to match their outputs, a step the report calls strong-to-weak distillation. So this model learned to reason by imitating a larger reasoning model rather than through its own reinforcement learning.

Same prompt, three models

Each task below asks for a prediction first. Make it, then use the tool to check it.

  1. A base model is trained only to continue text. On how many of the eight prompts will this base model continue the document instead of answering?

    Go through the prompts and read the base model’s column.

    One. On the Einstein prompt, the base model first wrote a second question (“Also, how did he manage to write his theory in a way that was not immediately understood…”) and then answered both. On the other seven it answered much as the chat model did. The Qwen3 report says its pretraining text included trillions of generated tokens of question-answering and instructions, so this base model has read many documents in which a question is followed by its answer. What a base model does with a question depends on its pretraining text. The base model also differs in when it stops: on three prompts it was still writing at its limit of 200 tokens, while the chat model ended every response by itself.

  2. On the letter-count prompt, the chat model answers that “strawberry” has one r. What will the reasoning model, the same parameters with thinking on, answer?

    Choose the letter-count prompt and read the chain of thought.

    Three, which is correct. In its chain of thought the model spells the word one letter at a time, stops to ask “Wait, is that correct?”, numbers the ten letters, and finds r at positions 3, 8, and 9. That took 319 tokens, each one a pass through the network; the chat model’s whole answer took 28. The base model answered “twice”.

  3. On the bat-and-ball prompt, the chat model writes out an equation and gets the right answer, 5 cents. What will the reasoning model do?

    Choose the bat-and-ball prompt and read the chain of thought from the start.

    It gives no answer. In roughly its first 350 tokens it sets up the same equation, finds $0.05, and checks it: $0.05 plus $1.05 is $1.10. Then it tries “another way”, takes the bat to cost $1.00, gets 10 cents, and spends the rest of its 3,000-token limit going back and forth between the two answers without closing its chain of thought. Checking and backtracking are behaviors the model learned from a larger reasoning model; in a model this small they do not always lead to an answer.

  4. Einstein’s Nobel Prize was not for relativity. Which of the three models says so?

    Choose the Einstein prompt, then the Mars prompt.

    None of them. All three explain why he won it for relativity. The 1921 prize was awarded “for his services to Theoretical Physics, and especially for his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect.” On the Mars question, which also has a false premise, the base model names a real Apollo astronaut, David Scott, and invents a walk on Mars in 1976; the chat model names Neil Armstrong and then adds that there is no record of a person walking on Mars in 1969; and the reasoning model names Mariner 2, an uncrewed probe. The small post-trained model has the same gaps and confusions as its base model. Post-training changed the form of its answers; it did not supply the missing facts.

What post-training changed

The three columns come from two sets of parameters, and the two sets are close. Measure the size of a set of parameters as the square root of the sum of their squares. Taken over all 310 parameter tensors at once, the size of the difference between the post-trained and the base parameters is 6.3% of the size of the base parameters; for a typical single tensor it is about 10%. A difference this small indicates that post-training started from the base model’s parameters; the report does not say so in as many words.Computed by tools/1-6/weight_diff.py; the result is in data/1-6/weight_diff.json. The median relative change of a single tensor is 10.2%, and the largest is 18.6%. The same knowledge, and the same gaps in it, show up in every column. What changed is the behavior: answering in the format of an assistant, stopping when done, and, with thinking on, writing a chain of thought first.

Empirical finding Zhou et al. fine-tuned a 65-billion-parameter base model on only 1,000 carefully chosen example prompts and responses, with no reinforcement learning. In their human study, its answers were judged as good as or better than GPT-4’s in 43% of cases, and than those of OpenAI’s text-davinci-003, a model trained with human feedback, in 65%. The authors concluded that almost all of a model’s knowledge is learned in pretraining, and that post-training mainly teaches which format and behavior to use.Zhou, Liu, Xu, Iyer, Sun, Mao, Ma, Efrat, and others (2023), “LIMA: Less is more for alignment,” NeurIPS; arXiv:2305.11206. They called this the superficial alignment hypothesis. Section 4.1 shows the same pattern stage by stage in another open model.

The letter-count prompt, “How many times does the letter r appear in the word strawberry?”, given to the three models. The base model answers “twice”, the chat model “once”, and the reasoning model, after a chain of thought (in gray) in which it spells the word and checks its count, “3 times”. All outputs are real and unedited.

Check

Does fine-tuning with human feedback mainly add knowledge to a model?

The evidence points to the second answer. Most knowledge comes from pretraining (Zhou et al. 2023), and the post-trained model in the tool repeats its base model’s gaps, such as the Einstein and Mars errors, while answering in a different form.

The chat and reasoning columns use the same parameters. Why did the reasoning model count the letters correctly when the chat model did not?

Each written token is another pass through the same network, and each pass can read everything written so far. Spelling the word one letter at a time turned a hard single step into many easy ones.

Takeaway

After 2022, three steps turned text predictors into assistants and reasoning models: fine-tuning on examples and human feedback (InstructGPT, ChatGPT), chain-of-thought prompting, and reinforcement learning on checkable problems (o1, DeepSeek-R1), which trains models to write long chains of thought. Post-training changes what a model does with its knowledge far more than the knowledge itself.
