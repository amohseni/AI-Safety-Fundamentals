The neuron

An artificial neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs, \(w \cdot x = w_1 x_1 + \dots + w_n x_n\), adds a constant called the bias, and passes the result through a fixed function called the activation function:

$$\text{output} = f(w \cdot x + b).$$

The weights \(w\) say how much each input counts, and the bias \(b\) shifts the result. The weights and the bias are the neuron’s parameters. Three activation functions appear on this page. The step function outputs 1 for a positive sum and 0 otherwise, which makes the neuron a perceptron (Section 1.2). The sigmoid, \(1/(1+e^{-z})\), rises smoothly from 0 to 1. ReLU outputs \(\max(0, z)\).

For example, with inputs \(x = (1, 0)\), weights \(w = (2, -1)\), and bias \(-0.5\), the sum is \(2 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 0 - 0.5 = 1.5\); the step function gives 1, the sigmoid gives 0.82, and ReLU gives 1.5.

The inputs where the sum is exactly zero, \(w \cdot x + b = 0\), form a line in two dimensions and a hyperplane in more.

    The edge sharpens, and the line moves toward the origin. Doubling \(w\) from (1, 1) to (2, 2) moves the line from \(x_1 + x_2 = 1.5\) to \(x_1 + x_2 = 0.75\).

    Use the left and right arrow keys on the picture.

    The line turns with the arrow and stays at right angles to it, and the shaded side is always the side the arrow points to.

  3. Choose OR and set \(w = (1, 1)\). Work out the sum at each of the four points. Which bias makes the neuron get all four right?

    Set the bias with its slider and read the table under the picture.

    Bias \(-0.5\). The sums \(w \cdot x\) at the four points are 0, 1, 1, and 2, so any bias between \(-1\) and 0 makes the sum negative only at (0, 0). Bias \(-1.5\) computes AND instead.

  4. Choose XOR and switch to the sigmoid or ReLU. Can some weights and bias get all four points right?

    Try for a while with each activation function.

    No. The best any setting does is one point wrong, for all three activation functions. The sigmoid is above 0.5 exactly where the sum is positive, and ReLU is above 0 exactly there too, so changing the activation function does not move the line.

What the arrow and the bias do

Theorem The line \(w \cdot x + b = 0\) is perpendicular to \(w\), and its distance from the origin is \(|b| / \lVert w \rVert\). So the direction of \(w\) sets the line’s orientation, and the bias, measured against the length of \(w\), sets its position. The length of \(w\) also sets how fast the sum changes as an input crosses the line, which is how sharp the transition in the output is. Multiplying \(w\) and \(b\) by the same positive number leaves the line where it is and sharpens the transition.

A neuron’s output depends on the input only through the sum \(w \cdot x + b\), which is the signed distance from the line, multiplied by \(\lVert w \rVert\). So a single neuron, whatever its activation function, can separate two classes only with a line, or in more dimensions a hyperplane. This is the perceptron’s limit from Section 1.2, and it is why networks stack neurons in layers (Sections 2.3 and 2.4).

Check

A sigmoid neuron has \(w = (1, 2)\) and \(b = -1\). You change it to \(w = (2, 4)\) and \(b = -2\). What happens?

The new sum is twice the old one, so it is zero at the same inputs: the line stays put. Away from the line the sum is twice as large, so the sigmoid goes from near 0 to near 1 over half the distance.

Takeaway

A neuron computes \(f(w \cdot x + b)\). The direction of \(w\) sets the orientation of the line or hyperplane \(w \cdot x + b = 0\), the bias and the length of \(w\) together set its position, and the length of \(w\) sets how sharp the output’s transition is. Whatever the activation function, one neuron divides its input space with one hyperplane.
