Gradient descent

Training searches for parameters with a low loss (Section 2.1). The loss \(L(\theta)\) is a function of all the parameters, and its gradient \(\nabla L(\theta)\) is the vector of its partial derivatives, one for each parameter. The gradient points in the direction in which the loss increases fastest. Gradient descent starts somewhere and repeatedly takes a step in the opposite direction:

$$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, \nabla L(\theta).$$

The step size \(\eta\) is called the learning rate. On this page the parameters are two numbers, \(x\) and \(y\), so the loss is a surface over the plane and you can watch the path the parameters take.

Computing the exact gradient of a training loss requires going through the whole dataset. In practice each step uses a small random batch of examples, so the gradient is a noisy estimate, and the method is called stochastic gradient descent (SGD). The playground imitates this by adding random noise to each gradient.

Two refinements are used in practice. Momentum keeps a running sum of recent gradients, \(v \leftarrow 0.9\, v + \nabla L(\theta)\), and steps along it, \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta v\), so steps build up in directions where the gradient keeps pointing the same way. Adam keeps running averages of each parameter’s gradient and of its square, and divides the first by the square root of the second, so each parameter’s step is scaled by the typical size of that parameter’s gradients.Kingma and Ba (2015), “Adam: a method for stochastic optimization,” International Conference on Learning Representations (arXiv:1412.6980, 2014).

    Just under 0.02. At 0.0195 the path reaches the bottom in 263 steps. At exactly 0.02 it bounces between the two walls of the valley forever without getting lower, and at 0.0204 it bounces further out each time and diverges.

  2. Set the learning rate to 0.01. Plain gradient descent then takes 515 steps to get within 0.0001 of the bottom. How many steps will momentum take with the same learning rate?

    Run plain gradient descent, then switch to momentum and run again. Try Adam too, at learning rates 0.01 and 0.1.

    103 steps. Across the valley, momentum swings back and forth and the swings cancel out; along the valley floor the gradient keeps the same sign, so the steps add up. Adam takes 631 steps at learning rate 0.01 and 96 at 0.1.

Why the learning rate has a limit

Theorem Across the narrow valley the loss is \(50\,y^2\), so the gradient in \(y\) is \(100\,y\), and one step sends \(y\) to \((1 - 100\,\eta)\,y\). The distance from the valley floor shrinks only if \(|1 - 100\,\eta| < 1\), that is, if \(\eta < 0.02\). In general, on a quadratic loss gradient descent converges exactly when \(\eta\) is less than 2 divided by the largest curvature. The steep direction limits the learning rate, and the flat direction, along the valley floor, then takes many steps. The more the curvatures differ, the slower gradient descent is, and that is where momentum and Adam help.

Hypothesis The noise in SGD may steer training toward wide, flat minima, as in the third task, and flat minima are thought by some to generalize better to new data. Keskar and colleagues found that training with large batches, which gives less noisy gradients, led to sharper minima and worse performance on test data.Keskar et al. (2017), “On large-batch training for deep learning: generalization gap and sharp minima,” International Conference on Learning Representations. The claim is debated. Dinh and colleagues showed that for common networks the sharpness of a minimum can be changed by rescaling the parameters without changing the function the network computes, so sharpness alone cannot be what decides generalization.Dinh et al. (2017), “Sharp minima can generalize for deep nets,” International Conference on Machine Learning.

Most large language models are trained with Adam or a close variant, such as AdamW, which handles weight decay separately from the gradient step.Loshchilov and Hutter (2019), “Decoupled weight decay regularization,” International Conference on Learning Representations. Section 2.7 explains how the gradient is computed for millions or billions of parameters.

Check

A loss surface is a valley with curvature 400 across it and 1 along it. With plain gradient descent, which learning rate is the largest of these that converges?

Convergence needs \(\eta\) below 2 divided by the largest curvature, here \(2/400 = 0.005\). Of the three, only 0.004 qualifies. At that rate the direction along the valley, with curvature 1, shrinks by a factor of only 0.996 per step, so it takes many steps.

A dataset has 10 million examples, and each minibatch has 1,000. One exact gradient costs about as much computation as how many minibatch gradient steps?

The exact gradient needs all 10 million examples; a minibatch needs 1,000, so one exact step costs as much as 10,000 minibatch steps. The minibatch estimate is noisy but far cheaper, which is why training uses it.

Takeaway

Gradient descent repeatedly steps against the gradient of the loss. The learning rate must be small enough for the steepest direction, which makes progress slow along flat directions; momentum and Adam speed up that progress. Training uses noisy minibatch gradients because they are far cheaper, and the noise changes which minimum training ends in.
