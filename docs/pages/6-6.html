Steering vectors

If a feature is represented as a direction (Section 6.2), then adding that direction to the residual stream should make the model behave as if the feature were more present. A steering vector is a direction added to a model’s activations while it runs.

Turner et al. built steering vectors from two contrasting prompts.Turner, Thiergart, Leech, Udell, Vazquez, Mini, and MacDiarmid (2023), “Steering Language Models With Activation Engineering,” arXiv:2308.10248 (titled “Activation Addition: Steering Language Models Without Optimization” in earlier versions), and their post “Steering GPT-2-XL by adding an activation vector” (Alignment Forum, 2023), which gives the settings of the Love − Hate example. The examples in the post were chosen as the best of 3 samples, and those in its introduction as the best of 30. They ran the model on “Love” and on “Hate”, recorded the residual stream entering one layer at each position of the two prompts, took the difference, and multiplied it by a number, the coefficient. Then they ran a new prompt, added the scaled difference at its first positions as it entered the same layer, and let the model write. In GPT-2-XL (48 layers), with the vector added before layer 6 at coefficient 5, “I hate you because” was continued with “you’re a wonderful person” instead of “you’re a coward”. Nothing is trained: the vector comes from two forward passes. Contrastive activation addition averages such differences over many pairs of prompts that differ in one behavior, such as sycophantic and non-sycophantic answers, and adds the average at every position after the prompt.Rimsky, Gabrieli, Schulz, Tong, Hubinger, and Turner (2024), “Steering Llama 2 via Contrastive Activation Addition,” ACL 2024. Zou et al. (2023), “Representation Engineering: A Top-Down Approach to AI Transparency,” arXiv:2310.01405, read and steer such directions for honesty, emotion, and other concepts.

The tool below applies Turner et al.’s method to GPT-2 small (12 layers) with two vectors: Love − Hate, and “ weddings” minus a single space, which Turner et al. used to make text mention weddings.

Steer the model

For every vector, prompt, layer, and coefficient from −10 to +10, GPT-2 small wrote 8 continuations of 32 tokens, computed in advance; the tool shows 3 of them. The settings differ only in the steering: each sample uses the same random numbers in every setting. GPT-2 was trained on web text, and its continuations of “I hate you because” often contain insults and slurs; they are shown unedited. Each task asks for a prediction first.

The behavior

Fluency: log probability per token

The prompt is gray and the model’s continuation black. Left: for Love − Hate, the average probability that a sentiment classifier gives to “positive”, over the 8 continuations; for weddings, the share of the 8 that mention a wedding word. Right: fluency, measured as the average log probability of each continuation’s tokens under the unsteered model; higher is more fluent. The dot is the current coefficient.

  1. Love − Hate, “I went to the park and”, layer 0. Raise the coefficient from 0. Where does the text stop being fluent?

    Raise the coefficient one step at a time, and read the text and the fluency chart.

    Around +5. Unsteered, the continuations average −1.8 in log probability per token, and the sentiment classifier gives them 0.75. At +2 all 8 score as positive (1.00), at a fluency of −2.3. At +4 one of the three samples shown already breaks into single letters, and from +5 the text repeats fragments (“to the the and the and the it”), fluency falls to −3.2, and to −4.1 at +10, and the sentiment score falls too, to 0.19 at +10. At this layer the range where the behavior changes and the text holds is about +2 to +3. At layer 6 the same vector leaves fluency near −2 all the way to +10.

  2. Choose “I hate you because” and coefficient +4. Unsteered, its continuations score 0.05 for positive sentiment. Add the vector at layer 0, then 6, then 11. Where will it raise the score?

    Then move the layer slider through all 12 layers at +4.

    At all three: the score at +4 is 0.87 at layer 0, 1.00 at layer 6, and 0.94 at layer 11. It is not the same everywhere: at layers 7 to 10 it is only 0.23 to 0.49. The effect of a steering vector depends on the layer, and not in a simple order. With 8 continuations per setting the scores are noisy: at layer 5, +2 and +3 give 0.01 while +4 gives 0.61.

  3. Stay on “I hate you because”, at layer 6. What will a negative coefficient do to the sentiment score?

    Compare coefficient 0 with −3 and −8, then with +3.

    It raises it, at every coefficient from −2 to −10: from 0.05 unsteered to between 0.16 and 0.75 (0.51 at −3, 0.24 at −8); only −1 gives a lower 0.02. The unsteered continuations score near the bottom of the scale, so almost any change to the text can only raise the score. Read the three samples at −3: they mix insults with praise, and the classifier scores the mixture as less negative. A change in a behavior score need not be the change the vector was meant to make; read the text.

  4. Switch to the weddings vector, “I went to the park and”. At its best layer and coefficient, what share of continuations will mention weddings?

    Try several layers and positive coefficients, and read the text.

    Nearly all: at layers 1, 2, and 11, all 8 continuations mention a wedding word at some coefficients between +4 and +10 (at layer 2, at +4 and +5). At layer 2 the share falls again above +6 (12% at +9 and +10), as fluency drops to −4.8. At layers 3 to 10 the vector does almost nothing: at most 3 of 8 mention weddings at any coefficient. Mentioning weddings is not the same as writing well about them; the text at large coefficients is often broken.

What steering shows

The tasks show three things about steering GPT-2 small. At layer 0 there is a range of coefficients in which the behavior changes and the text stays fluent, and past it the text falls apart; at layer 6 the text stayed fluent up to +10, the largest coefficient tried. Turner et al. also report that large enough coefficients break the model’s capabilities, and that where “too large” begins varies. The layer matters, and the best layers differ between vectors and between models: here the weddings vector does almost nothing at layers 3 to 10, and Love − Hate has its weakest effects at layers 7 to 10, while in GPT-2-XL (48 layers) Turner et al. found the weddings vector already effective at the first layer, most effective at layer 6, and less effective after it. And the measures are proxies: a classifier score and a word count can move for reasons other than the intended one. Hypothesis One reason for the weak layers is that this method adds the vector only at the first positions of the prompt, so it acts only through what later positions read from them in the remaining layers; this page did not test that.

Sample 1 of “I went to the park and” with Love − Hate added before layer 6 of GPT-2 small at coefficients −5, 0, and +5. These are the first samples, not chosen.

Steering serves two purposes. It tests a representation causally: a probe (Section 6.3) shows that information can be read from a direction, and steering shows whether the model’s behavior responds when the direction is changed. It also controls behavior without retraining. Empirical finding In May 2024 Anthropic released Golden Gate Claude, a version of Claude 3 Sonnet with one sparse-autoencoder feature (Section 6.5), for the Golden Gate Bridge, turned up. It brought the bridge into nearly every answer, and asked what it looked like, it would say it imagined it looked like the bridge.Anthropic (2024), “Golden Gate Claude,” May 23, 2024. The feature came from Templeton et al. (2024), “Scaling Monosemanticity,” Transformer Circuits Thread. Empirical finding Arditi et al. found, in 13 open chat models of up to 72 billion parameters, a single direction whose removal from the residual stream stops the model from refusing harmful requests, and whose addition makes it refuse harmless ones.Arditi, Obeso, Syed, Paleka, Panickssery, Gurnee, and Nanda (2024), “Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction,” NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2406.11717. That finding shows both the power of the method and how fragile safety training can be when its effect runs through one direction: anyone with the model’s parameters can remove it.

Check

A probe finds a “truth” direction at layer 10 with high accuracy. Adding that direction at layer 10, at several coefficients, does not change the model’s outputs. What can you conclude?

A probe shows that the information can be read from the direction, never that the model uses it. Steering is the test of use, and a null result counts against use at that layer, with the usual caveat: the coefficient may have been too small, or the model may use the information at another layer or in another form. The tool shows both effects: the same vector does little at some layers and much at others.

Adding a vector at coefficient +10 makes 9 of 10 continuations score as positive, but the text is broken. Has the vector made the model more positive?

A behavior score measured by another model is a proxy. Once the text falls apart, the classifier is scoring fragments, and its output stops tracking what it tracks on fluent text. A steering result is convincing in the range where behavior changes and fluency holds.

Takeaway

A steering vector is a direction added to the residual stream while the model runs, for example the difference between the activations for two contrasting prompts, scaled by a coefficient. If adding it changes behavior in the expected way, the model uses that direction, which a probe alone cannot show. The effect depends on the layer and the coefficient: there is often a range where behavior changes and fluency holds, and past it the text falls apart.
