What interpretability tries to do

Interpretability is the study of what a trained network has learned and how it computes its outputs. Methods differ first in what they access. Black-box methods use only inputs and outputs. White-box methods also look inside: at the network’s activations, the numbers computed at each layer for a given input, and at its weights, the parameters that compute those activations. Mechanistic interpretability aims at the most detailed account: the algorithm a network uses, stated in terms of the network’s parts.Olah et al. (2020), “Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits,” Distill, set out this program for image models. Bereska and Gavves (2024), “Mechanistic Interpretability for AI Safety: A Review,” Transactions on Machine Learning Research, survey the field.

Methods also differ in the kind of evidence they give. Correlational evidence comes from observing what goes with what: a pattern in the activations that is present whenever the input has some property. Causal evidence comes from changing one thing while holding the rest fixed and measuring what else changes. The two kinds of evidence support different claims about a network.

Why causal evidence matters

A probe (page 6.3) is a classifier trained on activations. Argument If it predicts a property with high accuracy, the property can be read off the activations, but the model may never use it: the information can be a by-product of other computations, and a flexible probe can learn part of the task itself.Hewitt and Liang (2019), “Designing and Interpreting Probes with Control Tasks,” EMNLP; Belinkov (2022), “Probing Classifiers: Promises, Shortcomings, and Advances,” Computational Linguistics 48: 207–219. Steering (page 6.6) and activation patching (page 6.7) change the activations and measure the effect on the output, so they can show that the model uses what the probe found. This is why the field prizes causal evidence: claims about how a network computes its outputs are claims about what makes a difference.

Argument For safety, interpretability is meant to answer questions that behavior cannot settle: whether a model represents facts it does not report, which goals or rules of thumb drive its outputs, and whether it behaves differently when it believes it is being tested. Each of these is a claim about what the model represents and uses, so each needs both kinds of evidence.

Takeaway

Interpretability methods differ in what they access (behavior, activations, or weights) and in the evidence they give. Observing activations gives correlational evidence that information is present; changing activations or weights and measuring the effect gives causal evidence that the model uses it. Claims about how a model computes its outputs are causal claims, so the field prizes causal evidence.
