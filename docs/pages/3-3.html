Next-token prediction and sampling

At each position, the last step of a transformer produces a score, called a logit, for every token in the vocabulary: 50,257 scores in GPT-2. Softmax turns the scores into probabilities that are positive and add up to 1. The result is the model’s probability distribution over the next token. During training, this distribution is scored with the log loss against the token that actually came next (Section 2.5).

To generate text, the model computes the distribution for the next token, one token is drawn from it, the token is appended to the input, and the process repeats. Everything a chat model writes is produced this way, one token at a time.

Two settings change how the token is drawn. The temperature \(T\) divides every logit before softmax:

$$p_i = \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j / T)},$$

where \(z_i\) is the logit of token \(i\). At \(T = 1\) these are the model’s own probabilities. A temperature below 1 makes the gaps between logits larger, which concentrates probability on the most likely tokens; a temperature above 1 makes them smaller, which spreads probability out. Top-p sampling then keeps only the most likely tokens, the fewest whose probabilities add up to at least \(p\), and draws among those in proportion to their probabilities.Holtzman, Buys, Du, Forbes, and Choi (2020), “The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration,” ICLR. They introduced top-p sampling, which they call nucleus sampling.

    Less coherent. At \(T = 1\) the continuations of “Once upon a time” are strange but mostly grammatical. Top-p 0.9 cuts off only a little of that tail at \(T = 1.5\); top-p 0.5 cuts off much more, and the text becomes readable again.

  3. At \(T = 1\), after which prompt is GPT-2 most sure of the next token?

    Switch between the prompts and look at the first bar.

    “Thank you very”: GPT-2 gives “much” probability 0.992. After “The capital of France is”, the most likely token is “the” at 0.085, and “Paris” gets only 0.032, about the same as “France”: GPT-2 small expects the sentence to go on in many ways, most of them not naming the city yet. After “My favorite food is” the 20 most likely tokens together hold only 29% of the probability.

  4. After “Once upon a time”, at \(T = 1\) with top-p 0.5, how many tokens can be drawn?

    Set top-p to 0.5 and count the bars that are not gray. Then raise the temperature to 2.

    Three: the comma (0.427), “the” (0.065), and “I” (0.041). The first two hold 0.491, just short of 0.5, so the third is needed to reach it. At \(T = 2\) the same top-p keeps all 20 bars and more beyond them, because temperature is applied first and spreads the probability over many tokens.

One answer is one draw

GPT-2 small’s ten most likely tokens after “The capital of France is”, at three temperatures, on the same scale. The order never changes, because dividing by \(T\) keeps the order of the logits. At \(T = 0.5\) the first token, “the”, has probability 0.397; at \(T = 1\), 0.085; at \(T = 2\), 0.005, with the rest spread over tens of thousands of other tokens.

Temperature and top-p do not change the model. They change only how a token is drawn from the same distribution. Empirical finding Holtzman et al. found that always taking the most likely token, or searching for the most likely whole continuation, gives dull and repetitive text, while drawing from the model’s full distribution often wanders into the long tail of unlikely tokens; cutting off the tail with top-p gave text closer to human writing by their measures. The greedy loop and the \(T = 1.5\) continuations in the tool are small examples of both failures.

Argument Because generation is sampling, any one answer from a model is a single draw from its distribution. A second draw from the same model with the same prompt can say something else. To learn what a model tends to say, one has to look at many draws, or at the probabilities themselves.

Check

As the temperature approaches 0, what does sampling become?

Dividing by a small \(T\) makes the gap between the largest logit and every other logit huge, so softmax puts almost all the probability on the most likely token. In the limit this is greedy decoding. As \(T\) grows large, the distribution approaches equal probabilities instead.

Two tokens have logits 2 and 1, and no other token is possible. At \(T = 0.5\), about what probability does the first token get?

Dividing by 0.5 gives 4 and 2, so the probability is \(e^4 / (e^4 + e^2) = 1 / (1 + e^{-2}) \approx 0.88\). At \(T = 1\) it would be \(1 / (1 + e^{-1}) \approx 0.73\).

You give a chat model sampling at \(T = 1\) the same question twice and get two different answers. What does this show?

The model and its distribution are the same both times; sampling drew different tokens. To learn what the model tends to say, look at many draws or at the probabilities themselves.

Takeaway

A language model outputs a probability distribution over the next token. Text is generated by drawing one token, appending it, and repeating. Temperature divides the logits before softmax: below 1 it concentrates probability on the likely tokens, and at 0 it becomes greedy decoding; above 1 it spreads probability toward unlikely tokens. Top-p keeps only the most likely tokens that together hold probability \(p\). Any one answer from a model is a single draw from its distribution.
