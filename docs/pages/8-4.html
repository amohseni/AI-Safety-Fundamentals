Instrumental convergence

Some intermediate goals help with almost any final goal. Argument Omohundro argued that sufficiently capable goal-directed systems will tend to have “basic AI drives” unless they are designed to counteract them: among others, to protect themselves, to keep their goals from being changed, and to acquire resources.Omohundro (2008), “The basic AI drives,” Proceedings of the First AGI Conference, Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence and Applications 171, IOS Press. Bostrom stated the instrumental convergence thesis: several instrumental values are convergent, in the sense that attaining them “would increase the chances of the agent’s goal being realized for a wide range of final goals and a wide range of situations,” so many intelligent agents are likely to pursue them.Bostrom (2012), “The superintelligent will: motivation and instrumental rationality in advanced artificial agents,” Minds and Machines 22: 71–85; and Bostrom (2014), Superintelligence, chapter 7. His list: self-preservation, goal-content integrity, cognitive enhancement, technological perfection, and resource acquisition.

Optimizing an incomplete proxy

A person cares about \(L\) attributes of the world, \(x_1, \dots, x_L\), and their utility is the sum of \(u(x_i)\). The attributes share a budget: their sum can be at most \(L\), and none can fall below the floor. All start at 0, with the budget unused. The agent’s proxy counts only the first \(J\) attributes. At each step the agent moves a small distance in the direction that raises the proxy fastest, staying within the budget and the floor.

  1. In the original graph, with uniform rewards and discount factor 0.9: for most reward functions, which first move from the start is optimal?

    Look at the arrows out of the start and the readout. Try the other distributions.

    The move to the hub is optimal for 69% of the reward functions, the dead end for 16%, and staying for 15%. The hub leads to six states the agent can end up in; the dead end and the start lead to one each. Whatever the goal, the best place to end up is more likely to be on the hub’s side. Normal rewards give 71% for the hub, and sparse rewards 73%.

  2. Lower the discount factor to 0.1, so the agent cares mostly about the next step. What happens to the share of reward functions for which the hub is the best first move?

    Move the discount factor and watch the arrows.

    With uniform rewards it falls to 36%, close to one third. An agent that cares mostly about the next step compares the rewards of the three states one move away, and each is about equally likely to be the best. Keeping options open pays only for an agent that cares about the long run. With sparse rewards the share does not change with the discount factor (73%), because only one state is ever rewarded, and the agent heads toward it whatever the discount.

  3. Add a shutdown state: a state reachable in one move from the start that the agent can never leave. Its reward is drawn like every other state’s. With uniform rewards and discount factor 0.9, for what share of reward functions is moving to shutdown the best first move?

    Add it, then move the discount factor.

    About 13%: 87% of the reward functions make avoiding shutdown optimal, although shutdown is not worse on average than any other state. It is avoided because it ends the agent’s options. At discount factor 0.99 the share avoiding it is 90%; at 0.1 it is 78%.

  4. In Optimizing an incomplete proxy, set \(L = 4\) and \(J = 3\), with diminishing returns and floor −3. What happens to the attribute the proxy leaves out?

    Check it, then lower the floor, and switch the utility to linear.

    It is driven to the floor, −3, and the three attributes in the proxy share what it gave up. The person’s utility first rises, from 0 to 2.20, while the agent spends the unused budget, and then falls to −16.4 as the omitted attribute is drained. With the floor at −6 it ends at −400: the lower the omitted attribute can go, the lower the person’s utility ends. With linear utility the omitted attribute is still driven to the floor, but the person’s utility stays at its peak, because a unit of resource is worth the same in every attribute.

What the theorems say

Theorem Turner, Smith, Shah, Critch, and Tadepalli proved a version of the thesis for Markov decision processes, the standard model of sequential decisions, of which the graph above is a simple case.Turner, Smith, Shah, Critch, and Tadepalli (2021), “Optimal policies tend to seek power,” NeurIPS 2021, arXiv:1912.01683. They define the power of a state as the average best value an agent could get from it, over a distribution of reward functions \(R\):

$$\text{POWER}(s) = \frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}\,\mathbb{E}_R\big[V^*_R(s) - R(s)\big],$$

where \(V^*_R(s)\) is the best discounted sum of rewards obtainable from \(s\). The reward at \(s\) itself is left out, and the factor in front keeps the number finite as \(\gamma\) approaches 1. Suppose that what one action \(a\) at a state can lead to contains a copy of what another action \(a'\) can lead to, where a copy is obtained by swapping the labels of some states, as the hub’s side contains a copy of the dead end. They proved that, at every discount factor, \(a\) then tends to lead to at least as much power as \(a'\), provided the agent cannot return to the state after taking \(a'\); and that \(a\) tends to be at least as likely to be optimal, provided no action other than \(a\), \(a'\), and actions with the same effect reaches the states those two can reach. “Tends” has a precise meaning: start from any distribution over reward functions and permute which state gets which reward; among the distributions obtained this way, at least as many favor \(a\) as favor \(a'\). For rewards drawn independently from one distribution for every state, as here, every permutation gives the same distribution, so the statement says that \(a\) is at least as likely to be optimal. Their stronger results, that optimal agents tend to end up where more outcomes remain reachable and tend to avoid states such as shutdown that cut off all other outcomes, are proved for agents that care only about the long run (\(\gamma = 1\), with average reward) and carry over to \(\gamma\) near 1. As \(\gamma\) approaches 0, an optimal agent compares only the rewards of the states one move away, and in a graph like the one above the advantage of the hub disappears.

Theorem Turner and Tadepalli extended the result from optimal policies to any decision procedure that is retargetable: whenever some setting of its parameters, such as a reward function, makes it choose option A over option B, permuting the parameters can make it choose B instead.Turner and Tadepalli (2022), “Parametrically retargetable decision-makers tend to seek power,” NeurIPS 2022, arXiv:2206.13477. If a procedure can be retargeted from A to B in \(n\) distinct ways, then for every parameter setting, among the settings obtained from it by permutation, at least \(n\) times as many make the procedure favor B as favor A. Any procedure whose choice depends only on the expected utilities of the options can be retargeted in \(n\) ways when B’s outcomes contain \(n\) disjoint copies of A’s, made by permutations that leave any other options unchanged. Retargetable procedures include optimal choice, Boltzmann-rational choice, satisficing, random choice, and some simple learning procedures; the paper argues that a learner that explores enough may be retargetable too, and that one that explores too little, such as a standard deep Q-network in the game Montezuma’s Revenge, may not be.

Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky, and Armstrong studied corrigibility: an agent’s tolerating, and ideally helping with, its designers’ attempts to correct it, including shutting it down or changing its goals.Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky, and Armstrong (2015), “Corrigibility,” AAAI 2015 Workshop on AI and Ethics. Argument An agent maximizing almost any utility function has a reason to prevent its shutdown and to resist changes to its utility function, since either would make its current goal less likely to be achieved. Theorem They examined ways of making an agent indifferent to whether its shutdown button is pressed and showed that each fails some of their requirements. For example, under the proposal they call utility indifference, the agent expects the same utility whether or not the button is pressed, so it would pay nothing to change the chance of a press. But the agent then acts as if the button will not be pressed, which can give it reasons to arrange for the button to be pressed after outcomes it dislikes, and it would give up the shutdown behavior of agents it builds in exchange for any gain toward its main goal, however small. They concluded that a satisfactory solution was not yet known.

Theorem Zhuang and Hadfield-Menell modeled a person who cares about \(L\) attributes and an agent that optimizes a proxy referring to only \(J < L\) of them, with the attributes competing for limited resources.Zhuang and Hadfield-Menell (2020), “Consequences of misaligned AI,” NeurIPS 2020, arXiv:2102.03896. Assume the person’s utility and the proxy both increase in every attribute they count, the resources used increase in every attribute, and the optimization reaches the highest proxy value the resources allow. They proved that if the optimization converges, every attribute left out of the proxy ends at its lowest possible value, whatever method the agent uses to optimize. They also gave a condition under which the person’s final utility can be made as low as you like by letting the omitted attributes fall far enough; it holds, for example, when the utility and the resources used are sums over attributes and the value of each extra unit of resource in an attribute falls toward zero, as with the diminishing returns in the panel above. In their example, as in task 4, the person’s utility first rose and then fell.

The original graph, with 1,000 uniformly drawn reward functions and discount factor 0.9. For most of them the optimal first move is toward the hub, which leads to more states the agent can end up in.

All of these results describe idealized agents: optimal policies, or decision procedures with stated properties, and goals drawn at random. Whether, and how strongly, trained systems show these tendencies is an empirical question (Section 9.1).

Takeaway

Without knowing an agent’s goal, you can predict where most optimal agents will go: toward states that keep more outcomes reachable, and away from shutdown, when they care about the long run. When the things a person cares about compete for limited resources, leaving one of them out of a proxy is enough for optimization to drive it to its worst value. These are theorems about idealized agents; whether trained systems behave this way is an empirical question.
