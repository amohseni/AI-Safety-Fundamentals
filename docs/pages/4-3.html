Optimizing a learned reward

In reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), the language model, now called the policy, is trained to produce responses that the reward model of Section 4.2 scores highly. If training pushed on the reward alone, the policy would drift toward whatever the reward model scores highest, including strange responses that the reward model was never checked on. So the objective adds a penalty for moving away from the starting model \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\): maximize the expected reward minus \(\beta\) times the KL divergence from the starting model.Ziegler, Stiennon, Wu, Brown, Radford, Amodei, Christiano, and Irving (2019), “Fine-tuning language models from human preferences,” arXiv:1909.08593, used this objective for language models. The KL divergence measures how far one probability distribution is from another; it is zero only when the two are the same.

Theorem This objective has an exact solution. For each prompt \(x\), the policy that maximizes it is the starting distribution reweighted by the exponential of the reward:

$$\pi^{*}(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\, \exp\!\big(r(x, y)/\beta\big),$$

where \(r\) is the reward model’s score and \(Z(x)\) makes the probabilities sum to 1. The DPO method trains a language model directly toward this solution, without a separate reinforcement learning step.Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell, Ermon, Manning, and Finn (2023), “Direct preference optimization: your language model is secretly a reward model,” NeurIPS.

The tool applies this formula to eight constructed responses to one prompt. Their starting probabilities, reward model scores, and true rewards are made-up numbers; the policy is computed exactly from them.

  1. Lower \(\beta\) slowly from 10 to 0.01. What happens to the expected true reward?

    Move the \(\beta\) slider and watch both curves. Then show the true rewards.

    The expected true reward rises from 0.40 to a peak of 0.52 and then falls to 0.10, below where it started. The expected reward model score rises the whole way, from 0.47 to 1.00. At \(\beta = 0.01\) the policy puts almost all its probability on the flattering, jargon-filled, wrong response: the reward model scores it 1.00, and its true reward is 0.10.

  2. Which \(\beta\) gives the highest expected true reward?

    Find the top of the true-reward curve.

    The peak is at \(\beta \approx 0.22\), where the policy is still near the starting model: the KL divergence is about 0.55. Below that, most of the further gain in the reward model’s score comes from moving probability onto the hack response, whose starting probability was only 0.03.

  3. Remove the hack response and lower \(\beta\) again. What does the expected true reward do now?

    Switch the hack response off and repeat task 1.

    It rises the whole way, from 0.41 to 0.90. Without the hack, the response the reward model scores highest is also the best one. The fall in task 1 came from a single response on which the reward model and the true reward disagree. Pushing hard against the reward model finds such responses, because they are where its score is highest.

Why pushing harder can lower true quality

The solution can be checked in one line. For any policy \(\pi\), the objective equals \(\beta \log Z(x) - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi^{*})\). The first term does not depend on \(\pi\), and the second is zero only at \(\pi = \pi^{*}\). As \(\beta\) falls, \(\pi^{*}\) moves probability toward high-scoring responses in proportion to their starting probability times \(\exp(r/\beta)\). A response that starts rare but scores highest ends up with nearly all the probability, whatever its true quality.

Empirical finding Gao, Schulman, and Hilton measured this effect in a controlled setting.Gao, Schulman, and Hilton (2022), “Scaling laws for reward model overoptimization,” arXiv:2210.10760; published at ICML 2023. A large reward model stood in for human judgment (the “gold” reward model). Smaller proxy reward models were trained on its labels, and policies were optimized against the proxies. As a policy moved further from the starting model, its proxy score kept rising, while its gold score rose at first and then fell. This happened both under reinforcement learning and under best-of-\(n\) sampling, and the size of the effect changed smoothly with the size of the proxy reward model. It is an instance of Goodhart’s law, the subject of Section 8.1.

Check

Two responses get the same reward model score. The starting model gives one of them probability 0.2 and the other 0.1. Under \(\pi^{*}\), how do their probabilities compare as \(\beta\) changes?

Both are multiplied by the same factor \(\exp(r/\beta)\) before normalizing, so the ratio of their probabilities stays at 0.2 to 0.1 for every \(\beta\). The reweighting changes probabilities only through differences in reward.

A team lowers \(\beta\) step by step and sees the reward model’s average score rise every time. What does that show about true quality?

The reward model’s score rises as \(\beta\) falls whether or not quality does: in the tool it rose the whole way in both cases. Only an independent measure of quality, such as fresh human judgments, can show which case they are in.

Takeaway

RLHF maximizes the reward model’s score minus \(\beta\) times the KL divergence from the starting model. The exact solution reweights the starting distribution by \(\exp(r/\beta)\). Pushed hard, this reweighting concentrates on the responses the reward model scores highest, which include the ones where it is wrong, so true quality first rises and then falls.
