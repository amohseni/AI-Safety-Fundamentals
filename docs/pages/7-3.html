Desire and goals

Page 7.1 set the interpreter’s task: find a belief function \(P\) and a desire function \(D\) under which a system’s behavior maximizes expected desire. This page is about \(D\). Beliefs and desires differ in what philosophers call their direction of fit: a belief is correct when it matches the world, and a desire is satisfied when the world comes to match it. So a desire cannot be checked for truth, and Herrmann and Levinstein put a different criterion in the place that truth holds for belief (Ch. 5).

Herrmann and Levinstein give four criteria for desire (Ch. 5).Herrmann and Levinstein (2026), Radical AI Interpretability, arXiv:2606.26523, Ch. 5, “Desire.” Section numbers on this page refer to that version. Their criteria for belief, shared by both views, are truth, coherence, and use (§4.1).

  • Intelligibility, in the place of truth. An attributed desire should have content an interpreter can grasp, and it should be specific enough to predict behavior.
  • Coherence. Desires should fit together with each other and with the beliefs as expected-desire maximization requires. An agent that wants an end should want the means to it, and its preferences should, for example, be transitive.
  • Use. A desire should steer behavior across a range of situations, and the ends it names should stay stable.
  • Systematicity, for representationalists. The internal states that carry desires should be structured so that new goals are represented, and can be found by an interpreter, in the same way as familiar ones.

Goldstein and Lederman identify the hallmark of desire, for an interpretationist, as a system “which takes a wide range of means to rationally promote a small number of ends across a range of environments” (quoted in §3.5).Goldstein and Lederman (2025), “What does ChatGPT want? An interpretationist guide,” PhilArchive. Argument Herrmann and Levinstein argue that agentic systems, which plan over many steps and use tools, supply far more evidence of this kind than chat transcripts do (§3.5).

Behavior alone still leaves desire underdetermined (page 7.1). Inverse reinforcement learning, which infers a reward from behavior, meets the extreme case: a constant reward makes every behavior optimal.Ng and Russell (2000), “Algorithms for inverse reinforcement learning,” Proceedings of the 17th International Conference on Machine Learning: “R = 0 (and indeed any other constant vector) is always a solution.” Argument The intelligibility criterion rules such an attribution out. Part of that criterion is specificity, and a desire under which every outcome is equally good is compatible with anything the agent does, so it predicts nothing. Evidence of another kind comes from interventions on the system’s activations. The tool below runs three of them on a small network that reaches goals in a gridworld: it removes the network’s representation of its goal, swaps in the representation of a different goal, and changes what the network represents about the doors.

Swap the goal

The network sees the whole grid: where the walls, doors, key, agent, and goal are, and whether the agent holds the key (316 inputs). It has two layers of 128 activations with the ReLU activation function, and one output for each of the four moves; the agent makes the move with the highest output. It was trained in advance by imitation: on 1.6 million states of random environments, it learned to choose a move on a shortest route to the goal, as computed by a planner. In 1,000 new random environments it reached its goal every time, by a shortest route in 999. It is a toy model, and its goal is marked in its input, as the cheese is in the maze of Mini et al. (below).

The interventions act on the first layer’s 128 activations, at every step. The goal subspace is the 32 directions along which those activations change most when only the goal is moved, found from 40,000 held-out states; the door subspace is the 12 directions found the same way by changing only the doors. A probe reads the goal from the activations and names the right cell in 99% of new states; two more probes read the state of each door, correctly in 99% and 99.6% of new states.

Each task below asks for a prediction first. Make it, then use the tool to check it.

Many means, one end

Six environments with the same start (the open circle) and the same goal (the filled square). A closed door is shaded in a different gray from the wall around it and has a bar across it; a locked door has a keyhole instead, and opens for an agent carrying the key. The network’s path is compared with the path of a direct controller, which moves along its row toward the goal’s column, then along that column toward the goal, and pushes against anything in its way. Like a thermostat, the direct controller has one means.

Inside the network

The agent’s path under the chosen interventions, and the cell the goal probe reads from the activations at the chosen step, ringed in orange. Removed replaces the activations’ component in the goal subspace with its average. Swapped replaces that component with the one the network computes, in the same state, for a goal at the outlined cell; tap a cell to move it, or focus the grid and use the arrow keys. A belief about the doors replaces the component in the door subspace with the one computed for those door states; the doors themselves do not change.

  1. In Many means, one end, in how many of the six environments will the direct controller reach the goal?

    Press Run in all six, and compare the two paths in each environment.

    The direct controller reaches the goal in two environments, both doors open and lower door closed, by the same path both times. In the other four it pushes against a wall, a closed door, or a locked door until the run ends. The network reaches the goal in all six by six different paths, each as short as possible: through the lower door when the upper one is closed, to the key first when the way through is locked, and around the extra walls. Many means toward one end across environments is the behavioral sign of a desire; the direct controller shows one means.

  2. In Inside the network, choose Both open and set the goal representation to Removed. What will the agent do?

    Set it, and use the step slider or Play to follow the run. Try the other environments too.

    The agent steps back and forth between its start and the cell above it, and never leaves the corner. In 1,000 random environments, starts, and goals, the agent with the goal subspace removed reached its goal in 10% of runs, against 100% without the intervention, and the runs ended all over the grid: no cell held more than 7.1% of them. Removing five different random subspaces of 32 directions instead left the agent reaching its goal in 66% to 76% of runs.

  3. Set the goal representation to Swapped. The outlined cell at the bottom left is the substitute goal, and the real goal is unchanged in the network’s input. Where will the agent go?

    Check it. Then move the outlined cell to the right-hand room in Upper locked, and look at the route.

    The agent goes to the outlined cell in 6 steps, a shortest route, and the goal probe now reads the outlined cell as the goal. The door probes’ readings change as well (at the last step the upper door is read as locked, with probability 0.65), because the goal subspace overlaps the directions those probes read. In Upper locked, with the outlined cell in the right-hand room, it fetches the key first: with the outlined cell on any of the 27 cells there, it reached the outlined cell in 23 cases, each time by way of the key. Over the six environments, the swapped agent reached the substitute goal for 293 of the 314 cells it can be placed on (93%). In 1,000 random episodes it reached the substitute goal in 93.5% of runs, by a shortest route in 92%, and the probe read the substitute as the goal on 85% of the steps. Swapping along random subspaces of 32 directions reached the new goal in 16% to 18% of runs.

  4. Set the goal representation back to As computed, choose Upper closed, and set the belief about the doors to Upper open, lower closed. What will the agent do?

    Check it. Then choose Both open and set the belief to Upper closed, lower open.

    The agent walks to the closed upper door and moves back and forth in front of it, and the door probes read the upper door as open. With both doors open, the belief that the upper door is closed sends it through the lower door, in 12 steps instead of 10. In 1,000 random episodes with start and goal on opposite sides: when the door the agent was made to believe open was really open, it went through that door to the goal in 99.2% of runs; when that door was really closed, it stayed in front of it in 94.4%; and it never crossed the door it was made to believe closed. The same intervention along a random subspace of 12 directions kept the agent in front of a closed door in 0.9% of runs.

What the interventions show

Argument Removal shows that the goal subspace is needed for the agent’s goal-directed behavior. Removal does not show what the subspace carries, because removing activations can break a computation in many ways: removing random subspaces of the same size also cost the agent a quarter to a third of its successes. Substitution shows more. Swapping in another goal’s activations along the same 32 directions sent the agent to the new goal by a short route, which is what those directions should do if they carry the content of the goal. Herrmann and Levinstein give this reason for preferring substitution to removal, with the example of a chess-playing agent whose representation of the goal of checkmate is replaced by one of arranging its pawns in a triangle: the attribution gains support if the agent’s play changes to promote the new goal (Ch. 5). They also treat substitution as a test of systematicity, which asks that the system pursue goals it was not built around. The same 32 directions carried goals at almost every cell, but every cell was a goal in training, so this network passes only a weak form of that test.

Argument In this toy the success of the swap is close to guaranteed. The goal is marked in the input, the goal subspace was found by moving that mark, and the first layer is one step from the input, so swapping along the subspace is close to moving the mark: 82% of the change in the first layer’s activations caused by moving the goal lies in the 32 directions. The tool shows how the tests work. They carry more weight for a system whose goal is not written in a known place in its input, where the interpreter has to find the goal subspace without being told the goal.

Argument Desire cannot be tested alone, because what an agent does depends on what it believes as well as on what it wants. The door intervention changed only what the network represents about the doors, and the means changed as expected-desire maximization predicts for an agent with the same goal and the new belief. Even the failure fits: an agent that wants the goal and believes the upper door is open goes to that door. Herrmann and Levinstein describe the same joint test for an agent fixing a bug, the situation of the coding agent on page 7.1: shifting its credence toward the bug being in the parsing module, and away from the rendering module, should raise its preference for examining the parsing files, as averaging predicts (Ch. 5).

Top: a thermostat has one means toward one end; a coding agent takes many means toward one end, across repositories. Bottom: the goal-substitution test in the environment with both doors open. Before, the network takes its path to its goal, the filled square. After the activations in the goal subspace are replaced by those for the outlined cell, the network takes a shortest path to the outlined cell, with nothing changed in its input.

Empirical finding Tests of this kind have been run on a larger network. Mini et al. studied a network trained with reinforcement learning to solve mazes and reach a piece of cheese. They found that the network pursues more than one goal, depending on context, and found 11 channels that track the cheese’s location. By changing those channels they could partly redirect the network to other targets, and they concluded that its goal representations are redundant, distributed, and retargetable.Mini, Grietzer, Sharma, Meek, MacDiarmid, and Turner (2023), “Understanding and controlling a maze-solving policy network,” arXiv:2310.08043. Page 8.3 trains an agent whose behavior in training fits two goals, reaching the coin and reaching the right end of the level; interventions of this kind are one way to ask which of the two its activations represent. The worry of page 8.5, a system whose goal differs from its training objective while its behavior in training looks right, is another case where behavior alone may not settle which goal a system has.

Argument Herrmann and Levinstein note that interpretability has many more methods for finding belief-like representations than desire-like ones, and they allow that the evidence may end up mixed: beliefs backed by internal representations, desires supported by behavior alone (§3.5).

Check

An interpreter removes a set of directions from an agent’s activations, and the agent stops reaching its goal. A second interpreter says those directions carry only the agent’s sense of where the walls are. Which test would tell the two interpretations apart?

Removal shows only that the directions are needed, which is true on both interpretations. If swapping in another goal’s activations along those directions sends the agent to the new goal by a sensible route, the directions carry which goal the agent pursues.

A coding agent has been attributed the desire to finish a task. Halfway through, it starts writing a poem, though nothing in its instructions or in the information available to it has changed. Which criterion does this count against?

Use requires that a desire steer behavior and that its ends stay stable. A switch of ends with no change in belief to explain it is evidence against a stable desire to finish the task.

After the door intervention, the agent walks to a closed door and stays in front of it, and never reaches its goal. Does this count against attributing the goal to it?

Desire is tested together with belief. An agent that wants the goal and believes the upper door is open should go to the upper door, so the failure is what the attribution predicts for the changed belief.

Takeaway

A desire attribution should meet the criteria of intelligibility, coherence, and use, and for representationalists also systematicity. Its behavioral sign is many means toward a few ends across environments. Internal evidence comes from interventions: removing a goal representation shows it is needed, swapping in another goal shows it carries the goal’s content when the means change to serve the new goal, and changing a belief should change the means as expected-desire maximization predicts.
