Learning from human preferences

People find it easier to compare two answers than to write an ideal answer or to score one answer on an absolute scale. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) therefore collects comparisons: a labeler sees two responses to a prompt and picks the better one.Christiano, Leike, Brown, Martic, Legg, and Amodei (2017), “Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences,” NeurIPS. Ouyang et al. (2022), “Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback,” NeurIPS, applied the method to GPT-3.

A reward model is a network trained to predict these choices. It is usually a copy of the language model with a single number as its output: the reward \(r\) of a response, often called its score. It uses the Bradley-Terry model of paired comparisons: the probability that a labeler prefers response \(A\) to response \(B\) is

$$P(A \succ B) = \sigma\big(r(A) - r(B)\big), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}.$$

Training maximizes the probability of the choices actually observed, so for each observed choice of \(A\) over \(B\) the loss is \(-\log \sigma\big(r(A) - r(B)\big)\). This is the log scoring rule of Section 2.5 again.Bradley and Terry (1952), “Rank analysis of incomplete block designs: I. The method of paired comparisons,” Biometrika 39: 324–345.

In the tool you are the labeler. The responses are constructed examples, not outputs of a real model: each answers a general-knowledge question, and each is either long or short, correct or wrong, confident or hedged, flattering or plain. Instead of a network, the reward model here scores a response by a weighted sum of those four features, and the weights are fitted live to your choices by the Bradley-Terry loss (with a small penalty on large weights).

    Label the pairs, then look at the weights.

    The weights describe your choices, whatever your reasons were. If you picked mostly by correctness, correctness gets the largest weight. We simulated a labeler who always picked the correct answer when exactly one was correct, and picked at random otherwise, over 20 random tie-breaks. Correctness got a weight between 2.0 and 2.5, and the features this labeler ignored still got weights as large as 1.7 in size. Twenty choices are few, and in a small sample features can line up with correctness by chance. The reward model cannot tell a chance pattern from a real preference.

For the careful labeler of task 1, it rose to between 0.4 and 1.3, and the weight on correctness fell from above 2.0 to between 0.7 and 0.9, because the simulated labels carry no information about correctness. The reward model fits all the choices it is given, from whoever made them.

What a reward model is

Argument A reward model is a model of human judgment, fitted to a limited sample of prompts and labelers. It learns whatever predicts the choices, including tendencies nobody meant to reward, such as a preference for longer, more confident, or more flattering answers. It can also be badly wrong on responses unlike those it was trained on. When the language model is then optimized against the reward model, these errors are what it can exploit (Section 4.3).

The Bradley-Terry model: the predicted probability that a labeler prefers \(A\) to \(B\), against the difference of their scores. Equal scores give 0.5; a difference of 1 gives 0.73; a difference of 3 gives 0.95. Only differences of scores enter.

Check

Suppose 10 is added to the score of every response. What happens to the reward model’s predicted preferences?

The prediction depends only on \(r(A) - r(B)\), which adding the same number to both leaves unchanged. Scores are therefore defined only up to a constant, and a reward model’s absolute scores mean nothing by themselves.

A reward model fitted to a team’s labels scores a long, wrong answer above a short, correct one. What is the most likely explanation?

The fit does what it is meant to do: it predicts the choices it was given. If those choices favored length over correctness, the reward model does too.

Takeaway

A reward model is fitted to pairwise choices with the Bradley-Terry model, \(P(A \succ B) = \sigma(r(A) - r(B))\), trained with the log loss. It is a learned stand-in for human judgment: it reproduces whatever predicts the choices, including the labelers’ biases and chance patterns in a small sample.
