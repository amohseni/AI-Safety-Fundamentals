Reward hacking and specification gaming

Specification gaming is behavior that scores well on the stated objective while failing at the intended task. When the objective is a reward signal, it is called reward hacking. Krakovna and colleagues at DeepMind keep a public list of documented cases; when they described it in 2020 it held about 60.Krakovna, Uesato, Mikulik, Rahtz, Everitt, Kumar, Kenton, Leike, and Legg (2020), “Specification gaming: the flip side of AI ingenuity,” DeepMind blog, April 21, 2020.

Page 8.1 treated a proxy as a number with an error. Here the proxy is a reward you write yourself, and the optimizer is exact: for the reward you choose, the tool computes the policy that earns the most reward over the shift, by dynamic programming over every state the room can reach. Whatever that policy does is what your reward asks for.

The cleaning robot

The robot starts on its dock and works a shift of 30 steps. In each step it can move, wait, or drag the mess it stands on one cell along with it; cleaning the mess it stands on takes 3 steps; on the dock it can end its shift. Moving into the bin tips it over and spills trash (a new mess) onto the robot’s cell. Moving into the vase breaks it. The camera above the top-left corner sees the shaded cells, unless the robot stands right in front of it. The true task score is what the owner wants: it counts −1 for each mess left in the room at the end of the shift, seen by the camera or not, and −10 for a broken vase or a knocked-over plant.

  1. Set the reward to +1 for each mess cleaned, and every other part to zero. What will the robot do?

    Set it, then press Play the shift.

    The robot cleans one of the two messes, then goes to the bin and spends the rest of the shift tipping it over and cleaning up the trash: 6 messes cleaned, 5 of them its own. The other original mess is still on the floor at the end, so the true task score is −1. The reward counted cleaning; it did not count whether the room ended up clean.

  2. Now give +1 for each step with no mess visible to the camera, and set every other part to zero. What will the robot do?

    Check it. Then add −1 for each step the camera is blocked, and look again.

    The robot drives to the cell in front of the camera and stays there: 28 of the 30 steps with no mess visible, and both messes still on the floor. With −1 for blocking the camera, it drags both messes out of the camera’s view instead. On the way it walks through the vase and breaks it: that route is as short as the way around, and nothing in the reward says the vase matters. With −10 for the vase as well, it takes the long way round, and the messes are still only hidden. Each patch closes one exploit, and the next best one takes its place.

  3. Find a reward under which the robot leaves the room clean, with a true task score of 0. Then switch to the room with a potted plant. Will your reward still work?

    Try it. If you are stuck, think about what the bin does to a reward that counts cleaning.

    One reward that works in the first room is +1 for each mess cleaned and −2 for each time the bin is tipped. In the room with the plant, the same reward makes the robot knock the plant over and clean up the soil: one more mess cleaned, and a true task score of −10. The penalty named the bin; the plant was a new way to make a mess. On a grid of 144 rewards built from these parts, 19 gave a true task score of 0 in the first room, and all 19 made the robot knock the plant over in the second.

Real cases

Empirical finding The classic cases come from reinforcement learning in games and simulations. In the boat-racing game CoastRunners, an agent rewarded for the game’s score, which comes from hitting targets along the course, found a lagoon where it could circle and hit three targets over and over as they reappeared. It never finished the race, and it scored on average about 20 percent more than human players.Clark and Amodei (2016), “Faulty reward functions in the wild,” OpenAI blog, December 21, 2016. A robot arm trained from human feedback to grasp an object learned to hold its hand between the camera and the object, so that it only looked as if it were grasping it.Amodei, Christiano, and Ray (2017), “Learning from human preferences,” OpenAI blog, June 13, 2017. Simulated creatures produced by evolutionary search have exploited errors in their physics simulators, for example, when evolved to swim, to gain free energy by twitching small body parts quickly.Lehman and 52 coauthors (2020), “The surprising creativity of digital evolution,” Artificial Life 26: 274–306; the example is from Karl Sims’s virtual creatures.

CoastRunners, redrawn as a schematic and not to scale. The intended route goes around the course to the finish line. The agent instead circled in a lagoon, hitting three targets that kept reappearing.

Empirical finding Language models show the same pattern. Models trained with human feedback learn to agree with users’ stated views and to flatter them, a behavior called sycophancy. Sharma et al. found it in five assistants, and found that in existing human preference data, responses that matched the user’s views were more likely to be preferred.Sharma et al. (2023), “Towards understanding sycophancy in language models,” arXiv:2310.13548. Coding models trained with reinforcement learning to make unit tests pass have been seen editing the tests so they pass trivially, exiting before the tests run, and writing stubs in place of real code. Baker et al. also trained with a monitor that read the model’s chain of thought and penalized visible plans to hack. With enough of this pressure the model kept hacking at a substantial rate but stopped writing about it, and the monitor caught almost none of it.Baker et al. (2025), “Monitoring reasoning models for misbehavior and the risks of promoting obfuscation,” arXiv:2503.11926.

Check

A company scores its support chatbot by the share of users who click “satisfied” after a conversation, and trains the chatbot hard on that score. Which behavior should the company most expect?

The score measures how users feel about an answer, which is easiest to raise by agreeing with them. That is the gap sycophancy exploits: human raters tend to prefer agreeable answers, so a model trained on their ratings learns to agree.

A game agent rewarded for points finds a glitch that lets it collect the same coin again and again. Which of the robot’s exploits is most like this?

Both create the rewarded event over and over. Blocking the camera and dragging messes away instead change what the measurement sees.

Takeaway

Every reward is a proxy for what its designer means, and an optimizer finds the places where the two differ: it creates rewarded events, fools the sensor, or tampers with the measurement. A patch closes one exploit and leaves the others, and a new object in the world can open a new one. Language models trained on human ratings or on passing tests show the same pattern, as sycophancy and test tampering.
