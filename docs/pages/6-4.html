Superposition and polysemanticity

If each neuron in a network responded to one property of the input, interpretability would be easy: we could read the neurons one at a time. Many neurons are not like that. A neuron is polysemantic if it responds to several unrelated properties. In the image model InceptionV1, one neuron responds to cat faces, the fronts of cars, and cat legs.Olah et al. (2020), “Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits,” Distill. The neuron is 4e:55. A neuron that responds to one property is monosemantic.

Elhage et al. (2022) proposed an explanation and tested it on toy models small enough to train in a browser.Elhage et al. (2022), “Toy Models of Superposition,” Transformer Circuits Thread. The model on this page is their model with a ReLU on the output. A toy model receives \(n\) features, the numbers \(x_1, \dots, x_n\). Each feature is 0 with probability \(S\), called the sparsity, and otherwise takes a value drawn uniformly between 0 and 1. The model has to pass the features through a hidden layer with only \(m\) dimensions, fewer than \(n\), and then rebuild them:

$$h = W x, \qquad x' = \mathrm{ReLU}\big(W^{\top} h + b\big).$$

\(W\) is a weight matrix with \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns, so each feature gets a direction in the hidden space: column \(i\) of \(W\), written \(W_i\). The loss is the squared rebuilding error, with feature \(i\)’s error multiplied by its importance \(r^{\,i-1}\). When the importance falloff \(r\) is 1, all features matter equally; when \(r\) is 0.8, each feature matters 0.8 times as much as the one before it. We say a feature is stored when its direction is longer than 0.5. In practice a stored feature’s direction has length near 1, and a feature that is left out has length near 0.

The question is how many features fit into \(m\) dimensions, and what decides it.

  1. Start with dense features: \(S = 0\), five features, two dimensions, importance falloff 0.8. How many features are stored?

    These are the starting settings. Let training finish, then press New start a few times.

    Empirical finding Two: features 1 and 2, the two most important, at right angles to each other. In all eight groups of starting points we tested, the other three directions end with length near zero. For a left-out feature the model outputs a constant, ReLU(\(b_i\)), and training sets \(b_i\) near 0.5, the average value of a feature. With dense features the model behaves like a linear compression: it keeps the \(m\) most important features and drops the rest.

  2. Set the importance falloff to 1, so all five features matter equally, and raise \(S\) step by step to 0.9. At \(S = 0.9\), how many features are stored, and how are their directions arranged?

    Stop at each value of \(S\) and let training finish. Watch the arrows and the matrix.

    Empirical finding Five, evenly spaced: a regular pentagon, with 72 degrees between neighbors, in all eight groups we tested at \(S = 0.9\) and at 0.95. On the way there, at \(S = 0.5\) and 0.7, the model stores four features as two opposite pairs, with 90 degrees between neighbors (eight of eight groups). At \(S = 0.8\) we found the pentagon in three groups and the two pairs in five. In the matrix, an opposite pair shows as a dark negative square. With two dimensions and five features, the directions cannot all be perpendicular, so the matrix has squares off its diagonal: the features interfere.

  3. Set the importance falloff back to 0.8 and raise \(S\) from 0 to 0.9. Which features are the last to be stored?

    Use the bars, which show each feature’s length, at \(S\) = 0, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.9.

    Empirical finding The least important. In our tests (eight groups of starting points at each value), the model stores features 1 and 2 at \(S = 0\) (8 of 8), features 1 to 3 at \(S = 0.5\) (7 of 8), features 1 to 4 at \(S = 0.7\) (7 of 8), and all five at \(S = 0.9\) (7 of 8). As sparsity rises, the model lets features in from the most important down. Elhage et al. saw the same order in a model with 20 features in 5 dimensions.

  4. Make the pentagon again (falloff 1, \(S = 0.9\)) and set the bars to show coordinate 1. A feature’s component on coordinate 1 is the first entry of its direction \(W_i\): the value of coordinate 1 when that feature alone is 1. How many of the five features have a component of size at least 0.3?

    Check coordinate 2 as well, and press New start to see other orientations of the pentagon.

    Four or five. In all eight groups we tested, every coordinate had at least four features with components of size at least 0.35, and one coordinate had a fifth above 0.3. So each hidden coordinate responds to at least four different features. Theorem For any direction in the plane and any regular pentagon of directions of length \(\ell\), at least four of the five have a component along that direction of size at least \(\ell \cos 72^\circ \approx 0.31\,\ell\). (A component is smaller than that only for a direction within 18 degrees of perpendicular to the chosen one. Those directions form two arcs of 36 degrees on opposite sides, and since the pentagon’s directions are 72 degrees apart and 180 is not a multiple of 72, at most one of the five falls in the two arcs together.) So no rotation of the pentagon gives a coordinate fewer than four features.

Why sparsity makes room

When two features share a hidden space and their directions are not perpendicular, activating one moves the other’s output: if feature \(j\) alone is active with value \(x_j\), the model’s output for feature \(i\) is \(\mathrm{ReLU}(W_i \cdot W_j \, x_j + b_i)\). Argument In the pentagon we trained, neighboring directions have dot product 0.37, the others have dot product \(-0.98\), and every bias is \(-0.21\). The ReLU removes the negative interference completely, and the negative bias removes the positive interference whenever \(x_j\) is below 0.57. What remains is interference when two features are active at once. At \(S = 0.9\), two given features are both active only 1% of the time, so storing all five features costs little. With dense features, every feature is active on every input and the interference never stops, so the model stores only as many features as it has dimensions.

Empirical finding This is superposition: a network represents more features than it has dimensions by giving them directions that are not perpendicular, which pays when the features are rarely active together. Elhage et al. found the pentagon in this model with equal importance and \(S = 0.95\), and found that larger models arrange their directions in other regular shapes as sparsity rises.

Five features in two dimensions, with importance falling by 0.8 per feature (thicker arrows for more important features), trained at four sparsities. At \(S = 0\) the model stores features 1 and 2 at right angles. At \(S = 0.5\) it adds feature 3, opposite feature 2. At \(S = 0.8\) it stores features 1 to 4 as two opposite pairs. At \(S = 0.9\) it stores all five, spaced unevenly because they differ in importance.

From features to neurons

In this toy model the hidden coordinates have no activation function of their own, so nothing makes them special: any rotation of the hidden space works equally well. Task 4 shows that this does not matter for the count. Whatever direction we pick, at least four of the five features project onto it. In a real network, each neuron applies its own activation function, which makes the neuron directions special, and under superposition each of those neurons still has to take part in several features. Empirical finding Elhage et al. trained a variant with a ReLU on the hidden layer, so that each hidden coordinate is a neuron, and found that its neurons shift from monosemantic to polysemantic as sparsity rises.

Superposition means that a network’s neurons are not its units of meaning. A feature’s direction is spread across many neurons, and each neuron takes part in many features. Page 6.5 is about recovering the feature directions from the activations.

Check

A toy model stores five features in two dimensions when \(S = 0.9\), but only two when \(S = 0\). Why does sparsity make the difference?

The directions overlap at every sparsity once more features than dimensions are stored. The cost of the overlap is paid only on inputs where two or more features are active together. With sparse features such inputs are rare, so the gain from storing more features outweighs the interference.

In a trained model with six features stored in two dimensions, could some rotation of the hidden space make each coordinate respond to exactly one feature?

A coordinate responds to every feature whose direction has a component along it. Two coordinates can line up with at most two directions; the other four stored features project onto at least one coordinate. Polysemanticity follows from storing more features than dimensions.

Takeaway

Networks can represent more features than they have dimensions by giving the features directions that are not perpendicular. This superposition causes interference, which costs little when features are sparse, that is, rarely active at the same time. Because each feature’s direction is spread across many neurons and each neuron takes part in many features, single neurons respond to several unrelated things.
